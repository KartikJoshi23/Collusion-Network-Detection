\section{The Project in One Page}
\label{sec:story}

\subsection{The problem}

Two very different crimes have the same hidden shape.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Money laundering.} Criminals move dirty money through many bank
        accounts so it ends up looking clean. No single payment looks wrong. The
        \emph{pattern} of payments is what is wrong.
  \item \textbf{Bid rigging.} Companies that are meant to compete for government
        contracts secretly agree who will win. Each bid looks normal. The
        \emph{pattern} of who bids against whom is what is wrong.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The one idea behind this project.}

Cheating like this is never done by one person alone. It needs a group. And a
group leaves a shape.

Draw the data as dots joined by lines. Each dot is an account or a company. Each
line is a payment or a bid. Now the crime is not hidden in any single dot --- it
is visible in the \emph{shape} the group makes.

So we stopped asking ``does this one payment look odd?'' and started asking
``does this whole connected group behave like a team that is working together?''
\end{keybox}

\subsection{What we built}

One system that reads both kinds of data, turns them into the same shape, and
hands an investigator a short ranked list of suspicious \emph{groups}. Each entry
comes with an explanation of why it was picked. A web dashboard shows the list,
draws the network, displays the evidence, and lets you ask an assistant
questions.

\subsection{Five things that make it different}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{One system, two crimes.} About 70\% of the code is shared. Only
        the data readers and the two warning-sign lists differ.
  \item \textbf{It ranks groups, not single items.} Investigators open cases, not
        spreadsheet rows.
  \item \textbf{Every alert comes with its reason} --- the exact small piece of
        network that triggered it, the name of the pattern, and a quote from an
        official warning-sign list.
  \item \textbf{We report ``how many can you check this week?''} If your team can
        review 100 cases, we tell you how many of those 100 are real. We never
        report plain ``accuracy'' --- Section~\ref{sec:metrics} explains why that
        number would be a lie here.
  \item \textbf{We publish our losses.} Where a simpler method beat us, we say
        so, with the numbers. See Section~\ref{sec:isitdl}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Is This Really a Deep Learning Project?}
\label{sec:isitdl}

This section exists because of a fair question: \emph{one of our simple
baseline methods beats our deep learning model on one test --- does that mean the
project failed?}

\textbf{Short answer: no.} But the reason is not spin, so here is the full
audit.

\subsection{First, what actually happened}

We built a deliberately strong \emph{simple} method to compare against. It is
called \textbf{XGBoost}: a classical (non--deep-learning) method that builds
thousands of small yes/no decision trees, where each new tree fixes the previous
one's mistakes. It is fast and very good at spreadsheet-shaped data.

On one specific test --- \emph{sorting individual transactions on the Elliptic
dataset} --- XGBoost scored 0.8104 and our deep model scored 0.4729. XGBoost won.
That is real and we publish it.

\subsection{Why XGBoost wins on that particular dataset}

This is the part that matters, and it is measurable rather than an excuse.

The Elliptic dataset gives each transaction 166 pre-computed numbers. Of those,
\textbf{72 were built by the dataset's authors by looking at each transaction's
neighbours} and averaging their values.

\pw{Somebody already walked around the network for you, wrote down what each
dot's neighbours looked like, and put the answer in the spreadsheet. The
network information has already been copied into the table.}

That is exactly the information a deep graph model normally has to discover for
itself. So on this dataset the simple method is handed the answer for free.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{We can prove this rather than just assert it.} We ran XGBoost twice: once
on plain features, once with extra neighbour information added on top.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
XGBoost without extra neighbour features & 0.8076 \\
XGBoost \emph{with} extra neighbour features added & 0.8104 \\
\midrule
\textbf{Gain from adding network information} & \textbf{+0.0028} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Almost nothing. Adding network information changed the score by three parts in a
thousand --- because the network information was \emph{already in there}.

That is the proof that Elliptic is a dataset unusually friendly to table-based
methods. It is not proof that graph learning does not work.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Now the important part: what XGBoost cannot do at all}

Sorting labelled transactions is \emph{one} of the jobs this system does. Here is
the full scoreboard, with the measured winner of each job.

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{5.0cm}p{4.4cm}p{5.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Job} & \textbf{Who wins} & \textbf{Can XGBoost do this job at all?} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Sort transactions when you already have labelled examples (Elliptic) &
\textbf{XGBoost} 0.8104 vs deep 0.4729 &
Yes --- and it wins. We report it. \\
\addlinespace
Sort firms when you already have labelled examples (Mendeley) &
\textbf{XGBoost + screens} 0.4558 vs deep 0.2808 &
Yes --- and it wins. We report it. \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Find cartels with NO labelled examples at all}, in a 163{,}000-node real
network &
\textbf{Deep} --- autoencoder 0.9137, deep combination 0.9225, versus 0.1237 for
the non-learning method &
\textbf{No.} XGBoost needs labelled examples. Here there are none. It cannot
even take part. \\
\addlinespace
Work in a country where you have never seen a single labelled cartel &
\textbf{Deep} (R-GCN) --- works on every market tested, 1.57$\times$ better than
guessing &
\textbf{No.} This needs a model that transfers what it learned. Trees cannot. \\
\addlinespace
Take what was learned about bid-rigging and apply it to money laundering &
\textbf{Deep} (frozen GraphSAGE) --- scores 0.1501 where guessing gives 0.065 &
\textbf{No.} The two datasets share no common columns. Only a learned
representation can bridge them. \\
\addlinespace
Show the small piece of network that caused the alert &
\textbf{Deep} (PGExplainer) &
\textbf{No.} Trees can rank which columns mattered. They cannot hand you a
sub-network to look at. \\
\addlinespace
Score the alert queue that the product actually ships &
\textbf{Deep} (combined model built on GATv2) &
\textbf{Not used.} XGBoost is not in the shipped system anywhere. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{What is actually running in the product}

We checked this directly rather than assuming. The file that tells the website
which results to serve (\code{eval\_outputs/serving.json}) points only at deep
learning outputs:

\begin{plain}
\code{alert\_queue\_ensemble} --- the queue, scored by the combined model whose
main member is GATv2, a deep graph network\\
\code{gnn\_gatv2\_focal} and \code{gnn\_gatv2\_focal\_multiseed} --- deep model results\\
\code{ensemble\_multiseed} --- the combined deep model\\
\code{gnn\_rgcn\_focal} and \code{gnn\_rgcn\_focal\_multiseed} --- deep model results
\end{plain}

\textbf{There is no XGBoost anywhere in the shipped product.} XGBoost exists only
as a measuring stick, which is the job we built it for.

\subsection{The honest verdict}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Where deep learning genuinely loses:} when you already have plenty of
labelled examples, and somebody has already copied the network information into
the spreadsheet for you, a tree model sorts individual items better than our
graph model does. That happened on two datasets and we publish both.

\textbf{Where deep learning is the only thing that works:} four jobs, and
XGBoost cannot attempt any of them.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Finding cartels with no answer key at all --- the 163{,}000-node study.
  \item Working in a country where we have never seen a labelled cartel.
  \item Carrying what was learned about one crime across to the other.
  \item Producing a small sub-network an investigator can actually look at.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{So:} the project is a deep learning project whose deep models power
everything that ships, and which is honest enough to publish the one axis where a
classical method wins. Deleting the comparison would have made the project look
better and be worth less.
\end{keybox}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Do not over-read this in the other direction either.} Our graph model
scoring below the ``blind guessing'' level on the Mendeley firms (0.2808 against
a guessing level of 0.358) is a genuine weakness, not a technicality. It is
listed as limitation~2 in Section~\ref{sec:limits}. We are claiming that the
project is sound, not that every model in it is good.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Glossary --- Every Word and Short Form We Use}
\label{sec:glossary}

Read this once and everything else becomes easy. Nothing in this report uses a
term that is not defined here.

\subsection{Words about networks}

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{4.1cm}p{11.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{What it means} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{Graph}, or network & A picture made of dots joined by lines. Dots are
things; lines are relationships. Everything in this project is one of these. \\
\textbf{Node} & One dot. A bank account, a payment, a company, a contract. \\
\textbf{Edge} & One line joining two dots. A payment, an award, a bid. \\
\textbf{Directed edge} & A line with an arrow on it. Money goes \emph{from} A
\emph{to} B. The arrow matters: money coming in is a different crime from money
going out. \\
\textbf{Degree} & How many lines touch a dot. ``In-degree'' counts arrows coming
in; ``out-degree'' counts arrows going out. A shop has many arrows in. A payroll
account has many arrows out. \\
\textbf{Neighbour} & A dot joined directly to another dot by one line. \\
\textbf{Subgraph} & A small piece cut out of a big network. For example, one
suspicious group and the lines between its members. \\
\textbf{Ego-network} & One chosen dot, plus everything within a few steps of it.
This is what the Graph Explorer screen draws. \\
\textbf{Motif} & A small repeating \emph{shape} that carries a meaning. Our nine
shapes are in Table~\ref{tab:motifs}. \\
\textbf{Community} & A cluster of dots much more connected to each other than to
everything else. Our alerts are communities. \\
\textbf{Clique} & A group where everybody is connected to everybody else. The
tightest possible cluster. \\
\textbf{k-core} & A number saying how deeply buried a dot is inside a dense
cluster. Higher means more buried. \\
\textbf{Clustering coefficient} & How often your neighbours are also neighbours
of each other. High means all your contacts know each other. \\
\textbf{Bipartite graph} & A network with two kinds of dots, where lines only
ever join different kinds. Firms and contracts: a firm bids on a contract, but a
firm never ``bids on'' another firm. \\
\textbf{Projection} & Turning the two-kind network into a one-kind network. From
``firm A bid on contract X'' and ``firm B bid on contract X'' we make ``A and B
bid on the same thing''. This is how we build \textbf{co-bid} links. \\
\textbf{Jaccard overlap} & A 0--1 score for how much two groups share. If two
alerts contain mostly the same firms, this is close to 1. We use it to delete
near-duplicate alerts. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{Words about learning from data}

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{4.1cm}p{11.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{What it means} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{Model} & A program that learns patterns from examples, instead of being
given fixed rules by a human. \\
\textbf{Deep learning} & Building models out of many stacked layers, where each
layer learns a slightly more abstract view of the data. The ``deep'' just means
``many layers''. \\
\textbf{Feature} & One measurable fact about a dot, written as a number.
``This account has 14 incoming payments'' is a feature. Models can only read
numbers. \\
\textbf{Label} & The known right answer for an example: criminal, clean, or
unknown. \\
\textbf{Training} & Showing the model labelled examples so it can adjust itself
to predict better. \\
\textbf{Validation} & A slice of data held back \emph{during} building, used to
decide when to stop and which settings to keep. \\
\textbf{Test} & A slice the model never sees until the very end. The only honest
score. \\
\textbf{Supervised} & Learning from labelled examples. \\
\textbf{Unsupervised} & Learning with \emph{no} labels. The model learns what
normal looks like, then points at whatever does not fit. \\
\textbf{Epoch} & One complete pass through all the training examples. \\
\textbf{Early stopping} & Stop training once the validation score stops
improving, so the model does not simply memorise. \\
\textbf{Overfitting} & When a model memorises the training examples instead of
learning the general pattern. Brilliant on old data, useless on new data. \\
\textbf{Seed} & The starting random number. Change it and you get a slightly
different model. We always build 5 and report the average, because a single one
can flatter you by luck. \\
\textbf{Hyperparameter} & A setting a human picks rather than one the model
learns: how many layers, how fast it learns, and so on. \\
\textbf{Embedding} & A short list of numbers summarising a dot's position and
behaviour in the network, produced by the model. \\
\textbf{Scoring}, or inference & Using an already-trained model to produce scores
for new data. \\
\textbf{Loss} & The number the model is trying to make small during training ---
its measure of how wrong it currently is. \\
\textbf{Focal loss} & A way of measuring wrongness that pays extra attention to
the rare and difficult examples, so the model cannot just ignore the 2\% of cases
that are criminal. \\
\textbf{Weighted cross-entropy (weighted CE)} & The simpler alternative to focal
loss: count mistakes on rare cases as worth more. \\
\textbf{Ablation} & Deliberately removing one part of the system to measure how
much that part was really contributing. \\
\textbf{Baseline} & A deliberately simple method kept as a measuring stick. If
the complicated model cannot beat it, the complication is not earning its keep. \\
\textbf{z-score} & Rescaling numbers so that average becomes 0 and typical spread
becomes 1. It lets you compare quantities measured on different scales. \\
\textbf{NaN} & ``Not a number'' --- the marker for a genuinely missing value. We
keep these rather than replacing them with zero, because zero is a claim and
missing is not. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{The methods we used, by name}

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{4.1cm}p{11.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Name} & \textbf{What it is} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{GNN} --- Graph Neural Network & A deep learning model that works directly
on dots-and-lines data. Each dot repeatedly asks its neighbours what they look
like and updates its own description. Explained fully in
Section~\ref{sec:algos}. \\
\textbf{GAT} --- Graph Attention Network & A GNN where each dot learns \emph{how
much to listen} to each neighbour rather than treating them all equally. \\
\textbf{GATv2} & The fixed, second version of GAT. We use this one. The original
has a known flaw, explained in Section~\ref{sec:algos}. \\
\textbf{GraphSAGE} & A GNN that samples a fixed number of neighbours instead of
looking at all of them, so it works on very large networks and on dots it has
never seen before. \\
\textbf{R-GCN} --- Relational Graph Convolutional Network & A GNN that learns a
separate rule for each \emph{type} of line. Necessary for contracts, where ``was
awarded'', ``bid on'' and ``shares a director with'' mean completely different
things. \\
\textbf{Autoencoder} & A model trained to squash something down and then rebuild
it. Practising on mostly-normal data makes it good at rebuilding normal things,
so whatever it rebuilds badly is unusual. Needs no labels. \\
\textbf{GAE} --- Graph Autoencoder & An autoencoder that rebuilds the network's
connections. \\
\textbf{DOMINANT} & An autoencoder that rebuilds both the connections and the
dots' own numbers. \\
\textbf{XGBoost} & A classical, non-deep method that builds thousands of small
decision trees, each correcting the last. Very strong on spreadsheet data. We use
it as our measuring stick. \\
\textbf{Leiden} & The method that finds clusters --- groups more connected inside
than outside. \\
\textbf{Louvain} & The older, more famous clustering method. We rejected it;
Section~\ref{sec:algos} says why. \\
\textbf{Isotonic regression} & The maths used to convert each model's private
scoring scale into real probabilities. It only assumes ``higher stays higher'',
nothing about the shape. \\
\textbf{PGExplainer} & The method that finds which few lines actually caused an
alert. Trains once, then explains the whole queue cheaply. \\
\textbf{GNNExplainer} & The older alternative. We tested it, it failed, we
replaced it. Numbers in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}. \\
\textbf{NMS} --- Non-Maximum Suppression & Going down the ranked list and deleting
any alert that mostly repeats one already accepted. \\
\textbf{SMOTE / GraphSMOTE} & Methods that invent extra fake examples of the rare
class. We rejected them --- Section~\ref{sec:algos}. \\
\textbf{PNA, GIN} & Two other GNN designs published as strong on money-laundering
data. Listed as future work; we lack the hardware. \\
\textbf{TGN, TGAT} & GNNs that treat time as continuous rather than in steps.
Future work, not claimed. \\
\textbf{PyG} --- PyTorch Geometric & The software library we build our graph
models with. \\
\textbf{PyGOD} & A library for finding odd dots. We tried it, it did not work for
our case, we wrote our own. \\
\textbf{GADBench} & A published 2023 study that found tree methods often beat
graph networks. It is the reason we built the XGBoost measuring stick. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\subsection{Words specific to this project}

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{4.1cm}p{11.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{What it means} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{IR} --- Intermediate Representation & Our one shared data format. Every
dataset is converted into the same four files, so everything after that point
works on both crime types without changes. \\
\textbf{Adapter} & The small piece of code that converts one raw dataset into the
IR. \\
\textbf{Alert} & One suspicious group, with a rank, a risk score and an
explanation. This is what an investigator opens. \\
\textbf{Alert queue} & The ranked list of alerts, cut off at whatever number of
cases the team can review. \\
\textbf{Budget}, written $k$ & How many cases the team can actually review. All
our headline numbers are quoted at a budget. \\
\textbf{Explanation bundle} & The file attached to every alert: the small
sub-network, the pattern name, the evidence, the official warning-sign quote, and
the fixed ethics wording. \\
\textbf{Motif matcher} & Plain rule-based code that names the shape it finds. It
does not use the neural network at all, so it acts as an independent check on
it. \\
\textbf{Red flag} & An officially published warning sign. We quote two lists ---
see FATF and OECD below. \\
\textbf{FATF} --- Financial Action Task Force & The international body that
publishes the standard warning signs for money laundering. We cite its indicators
in financial alerts. \\
\textbf{OECD} --- Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development & The
international body whose published checklist lists the standard warning signs for
bid rigging. We cite it in procurement alerts. \\
\textbf{Floor} & The simplest possible scoring method, with no learning at all.
Kept so we always know what ``zero cleverness'' achieves. \\
\textbf{Calibration} & Rescaling each model's output so the numbers mean the same
thing before we combine them. Without it, combining models destroys them --- we
measured this. \\
\textbf{Ensemble}, or fusion & Combining several models' scores into one. \\
\textbf{Roll-up} & Turning scores for individual dots into a score for a whole
group. \\
\textbf{Leakage} & When information from the test period accidentally reaches the
model during training. It inflates every score and invalidates the results. \\
\textbf{Strict-inductive split} & Our anti-leakage rule: during training the model
may not even see the \emph{lines} belonging to the test period, not just the
answers. \\
\textbf{As-of discipline} & Every feature is computed using only what was known
at that moment. Never the future. \\
\textbf{LOCO} --- Leave One Country Out & Train on six countries, test on the
seventh, then rotate so each country takes a turn. It answers ``will this work in
a country where we have never seen a single labelled cartel?'' \\
\textbf{LOMO} --- Leave One Market Out & Exactly the same idea, but rotating

\subsection{The rare-criminal problem}

In the Elliptic data, only about 2 in every 100 payments are criminal. This is
the central difficulty of the whole project.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A model can be right 98\% of the time by simply never flagging anything.
  \item The model sees roughly 50 innocent examples for every guilty one, so the
        easiest thing for it to do is ignore the guilty ones completely.
  \item The usual scoring numbers look excellent while the system is useless.
\end{itemize}

We answer it four ways:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Focal loss} --- makes the model pay attention to the rare cases.
  \item \textbf{Budget-based measuring} instead of accuracy.
  \item \textbf{The guessing level printed beside every score}, always.
  \item \textbf{Planting extra known-fake cases}, so the model sees more
        examples without us distorting the real data.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{How does focal loss actually help? (The follow-up question, answered.)}

Ordinary training treats every mistake as equally important. With 50 innocent
examples for every guilty one, the fastest way for the model to reduce its total
error is to get very good at saying ``fine'' --- the rare cases are a rounding
error in its score, so it learns to ignore them.

Focal loss changes the arithmetic. Once the model is already confident and
correct about an easy case, that case's contribution is scaled \emph{down}
sharply. The easy majority stops shouting, so the difficult rare cases --- the
ones that actually carry the signal --- become the loudest thing left, and the
model is forced to spend its capacity on them.

\pw{A teacher who keeps re-marking the questions the class already gets right
learns nothing about the class. Focal loss tells the model to stop re-marking
the easy ones.}

\textbf{And did it work?} Honestly: decisively on one model family and
inconclusively on another. Section~\ref{sec:tuning} reports both.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{How the data is split into training and testing}

This part protects the credibility of every number in this report.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Rule 1 --- split by time, never at random.}

Crime data is a timeline. If you shuffle it and pick test cases at random, the
model ends up learning from next year in order to predict last year. That is
impossible in real life, and it makes every score look far better than it is.
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection*{``But everyone says 80/20 is best --- why aren't you at 80/20?''}

This is the first question people ask, and it deserves a measured answer rather
than an appeal to convention. We tested it.

\textbf{First, where 80/20 comes from.} It is advice for data you can shuffle:
a pile of photos, a table of customers. Any row is as good as any other, so you
just need a big enough test sample.

Our data is not like that. It is one connected network moving through time.
Shuffling it would let the future leak into the past, which Rule 1 forbids.

So the split has to be a \emph{moment} in time --- and the percentage is
whatever happens to fall either side of that moment.

\textbf{Second, what 80/20 would actually cost.} We computed every possible
temporal cut on Elliptic++. The cut closest to 80/20 is after time step 40.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Split} & \textbf{Train / Test} & \textbf{Labelled test items} & \textbf{Criminal test items} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Ours} (cut at 34) & 66.9 / 33.1 & 16{,}670 & \textbf{1{,}083} \\
80/20 (cut at 40) & 79.3 / 20.7 & 9{,}973 & \textbf{524} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{An 80/20 split would throw away 52\% of the criminal cases we are able
to measure on.}

That is the entire argument. When only about 6 in 100 items are criminal, the
test set's value is set by \emph{how many criminals it contains}, not how many
rows it has. Halving your positives roughly doubles the noise in every number
you report --- you would be making the evaluation weaker in order to hit a round
number that was never meant for this kind of data.
\end{keybox}

\textbf{Third, and worse: 80/20 lands in a hole.} Look at what the late period
actually contains:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Time step} & \textbf{Criminal} & \textbf{Labelled} & \textbf{Criminal share} \\
\midrule
42 & 239 & 2{,}154 & 11.10\% \\
\textbf{43} & \textbf{24} & 1{,}370 & \textbf{1.75\%} \quad $\leftarrow$ regime change \\
44 & 24 & 1{,}591 & 1.51\% \\
45 & \textbf{5} & 1{,}221 & 0.41\% \\
46 & \textbf{2} & 712 & 0.28\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A dark marketplace was shut down around step 43, and criminal activity in the
data falls off a cliff. Steps 45 and 46 contain \textbf{five} and \textbf{two}
criminal items. An 80/20 test set is dominated by these almost-empty periods, so
its score would be decided by a handful of items and would swing wildly for
reasons that have nothing to do with the model.

\textbf{Fourth --- the reason that outranks all of the above.} Cutting at step 34
is the \textbf{published convention} every other paper on this dataset uses. Our
numbers are directly comparable with theirs; an 80/20 cut would make them
comparable with nothing. In a research project that is worth more than any
tuning gain.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{The short answer, for a presentation.}

``80/20 is a rule for shuffled independent data. Ours is a network over time, so
we split at a date, not at a percentage --- and the percentage is whatever falls
either side. We also checked what 80/20 would cost: it would discard 52\% of the
criminal cases we can measure on, and put the test set inside a period where two
of the time steps contain five and two criminals respectively. Our cut also
happens to be the standard used by every published paper on this dataset, so our
numbers can be compared with theirs. More test data is a feature here, not
waste.''
\end{plain}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{And the same logic on the others.} Mendeley's cut is 2013/2014 because
that is where the cartel cases and the contract record both give enough data
either side; AMLworld's near-50/50 arises because its accounts nearly all appear
in the first few minutes, so any cut later than that leaves almost no
\emph{new} accounts to test on. In every case the boundary is chosen for a
reason about the data, and the percentage is reported, never targeted.
\end{plain}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The percentage that actually matters is not the row count.}

On Elliptic++ only about a quarter of payments carry an answer at all, and the
proportion that are criminal \emph{changes between the two sides}:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Labelled} & \textbf{Criminal share} & \textbf{Unknown} \\
\midrule
Train (steps 1--34) & 29{,}894 & 11.58\% & 78.1\% \\
Test (steps 35--49) & 16{,}670 & \textbf{6.50\%} & 75.3\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Criminal cases are \emph{almost half as common} in the test period as in the
training period. The world moved between the two halves --- which is exactly the
shift this project is built to measure, and a large part of why validation
scores (0.93--0.95) are so much higher than test scores (0.42--0.55). Anyone
quoting a single train/test percentage without this table is telling less than
half the story.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Rule 2 --- hide the future connections, not just the future answers.}

\pw{Suppose you are predicting who will become friends next year. Covering up
next year's \emph{names} is not enough --- if you can still see next year's
friendship \emph{lines}, you already know the answer.}

So during training our model cannot see any line belonging to the test period.
We checked this on real data: the splitter withholds exactly 77{,}512 test-period
lines on Elliptic++. Published work shows that skipping this rule can inflate
scores by around 39.5 points on a common measure.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Rule 3 --- every feature uses only what was known at the time.}
Checked on real data: the ``as of step 34'' view contains 136{,}265 dots and is
exactly identical to the properly cut-off network.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Rule 4 --- the computer checks these rules, not a person.}
A set of 25 automatic tests runs every time anyone changes the code. If a change
lets future information leak in, the change is rejected.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{How we cleaned the data}

We deliberately did \textbf{not} ``clean'' it in the usual sense. No deleting
awkward rows. No filling blanks with averages. In fraud work the strange rows are
often the interesting ones, and inventing values would mean inventing evidence.
What we actually did:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Check before using.} Every file gets a fingerprint (SHA-256)
        compared against a stored one. For Mendeley, our fingerprint matches the
        publisher's own official fingerprint.
  \item \textbf{Convert, do not alter.} Each reader maps the raw columns into our
        shared format. Values are carried across unchanged, and anything that
        does not fit the format is kept in a spare field rather than thrown away.
  \item \textbf{Leave real gaps as gaps.} A Mendeley row with no buyer simply
        produces no ``buyer'' line --- we do not invent one. Where losing-bid data
        does not exist for a country, we \emph{switch those features off} rather
        than fill them with zero. A zero would be a lie: it says ``they never bid
        together'', when the truth is ``we do not know''.
  \item \textbf{Keep the unknowns in the network.} 77\% of Elliptic dots have no
        answer. We do not delete them. They are not used for marking the model
        right or wrong, but they \emph{do} pass messages, because they are the
        structure that the crime is made of.
  \item \textbf{Missing numbers stay missing.} XGBoost understands genuine gaps.
        For a dot with no neighbours, the neighbour-average is recorded as
        missing, not as 0.
  \item \textbf{Rescale using training data only, then freeze it.} Features are
        rescaled so they are comparable. \textbf{The rescaling amounts are worked
        out on training data and then locked.} This turned out to matter enormously
        --- see Section~\ref{sec:tuning}.
  \item \textbf{Remove duplicate alerts, not duplicate data.} Duplicate removal
        happens at the very end, on the alert list, where it is a reporting
        choice rather than a change to the data.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Two rules that came out of an internal review:}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Awards first.} Because losing bids are missing in many countries,

  \item \textbf{One shared set of shape-features.} Degrees, shape counts,

        rescaled per network. \textbf{This shared channel is the only reason
        moving between crime types is possible at all}, since Bitcoin payments
        and European contracts share no columns whatsoever.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Methods --- What They Are and Why We Picked Them}
\label{sec:algos}

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks --- the heart of it}

\begin{plain}
\pw{Every dot sends a short description of itself to its neighbours, and listens
to theirs. It then updates its own description to include what it heard. Do that
twice and every dot's description now contains information about its neighbours
and its neighbours' neighbours.}

The result: a dot is described by \emph{the company it keeps}. That is exactly
what we need, because a laundering account looks perfectly ordinary on its own
and only looks wrong once you see who it deals with.
\end{plain}

We use two rounds of this, so each dot sees two steps out.

\subsubsection*{GATv2 --- learning who to listen to (our main money-side model)}

\pw{Not every neighbour matters equally. If a dot has 200 neighbours but only 3
are important, it should be able to concentrate on those 3. Attention is the
mechanism that lets it learn which ones to weight heavily.}

A useful side-effect: those weights are readable afterwards, so they double as
evidence for which links drove a score.

\textbf{Why the second version and not the original?} The original GAT has a
proven flaw: every dot ends up ranking the importance of its neighbours in the
same order, regardless of which dot is doing the looking. GATv2 fixes that, and
costs exactly the same to run. Choosing the old one would be strictly worse for
no saving.

\subsubsection*{GraphSAGE --- the one that scales}

\pw{Instead of listening to all neighbours --- impossible if a dot has 100{,}000
--- listen to a random handful. This works on huge networks, and it works on dots
the model has never seen before.}

Ours listens separately to incoming and outgoing lines.

\subsubsection*{R-GCN --- handling different kinds of link (our main contract-side model)}

\pw{``Firm won this contract'', ``firm bid on this contract'' and ``firm shares a
director with that firm'' are three completely different relationships. R-GCN
learns a separate rule for each kind of line, instead of pretending all lines are
the same.}

\subsubsection*{Direction --- a small decision with a big effect}

Money and awards flow in a direction, and \emph{the direction is the crime}: a
funnel and a sprinkler are opposite offences. So every model is given both
directions plus a marker saying which is which. Our tests show this is the single
most important ingredient in the whole system (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Autoencoders --- spotting oddities with no answer key}

\pw{Train a model to squash each dot down to a few numbers and then rebuild it.
Because it practises mostly on normal things, it gets good at rebuilding normal
things. So whatever it rebuilds \emph{badly} is unusual --- and unusual is worth a
look. The key point: this needs no answer key at all.}

We built two: \textbf{GAE}, which rebuilds the connections, and
\textbf{DOMINANT}, which rebuilds the connections and the dot's own numbers.

\textbf{Why this matters so much:} real deployments usually have no answer key.
Our largest dataset has none by design, and this is the only family of methods
that works there. It is also where deep learning wins most clearly --- see
Section~\ref{sec:isitdl}.

\subsection{The floor --- deliberately stupid, deliberately kept}

The simplest possible scorer: count structural things, rescale them, average the
high ones. No learning whatsoever.

\textbf{Why keep something so basic?} So we always know what ``no cleverness at
all'' scores. It is our reality check. On one pattern it actually beats
everything else, which is worth knowing --- and on the big planted-cartel study it
scores 0.1237 where the deep models score 0.91, which tells us the deep models
are genuinely doing something.

\subsection{Calibration --- making scores comparable before combining them}

\begin{plain}
\pw{Model A says ``0.9'' and model B says ``0.4''. You cannot simply average
these. A might be an optimist who says 0.9 about everything. First you have to
translate each model's private scale into real probabilities. Only then can you
combine them.}
\end{plain}

\textbf{This is not a preference. It is something we measured.} Combining the
models without translating first drops the score from \textbf{0.4434 to 0.0511}
--- near-total collapse. It happens every single time, on every attempt.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why does it collapse so badly? (The follow-up question, answered.)}

Our five members are wildly unequal. The supervised model scores about 0.47; the
autoencoders and the floor score 0.04--0.06, which is \emph{at or below blind
guessing}.

Averaging raw rankings gives all five an equal vote. Four of the five voters are
no better than random, so their noise simply outvotes the one member that knows
anything, and the combined ranking ends up close to random too.

Converting each member's output into a real probability changes that. The weak
members end up saying ``about 5\% for everything'', because that is genuinely
all they know. Saying the same thing about everything barely moves a ranking.
Meanwhile the strong member's scores spread out and take over.

\emph{Being uninformative becomes self-silencing} --- and nobody had to pick
weights by hand.

\textbf{So why keep the weak members at all?} Because on data with no answer key
they are the only thing that works --- the entire 163{,}000-node study runs on
them. Calibration is what makes it safe to carry them.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Leiden --- forming the groups}

\pw{Leiden finds clusters that are tightly connected inside and loosely connected
to everything else. We use it to turn ``suspicious dots'' into ``suspicious
groups'', because an investigator opens a case, not a row.}

\textbf{Why not Louvain, the more famous one?} Louvain can produce a ``group''
whose members are not actually connected to each other --- two unrelated fragments
filed together. For us that would be an incoherent case file. Leiden guarantees
the members are genuinely connected.

\subsection{Removing duplicates, and capping size}

Go down the ranked list. If a new alert is mostly the same firms as one already
accepted, drop it. Also refuse any alert with more than 100 members.

\pw{Without the first rule, one big cluster appears ten times in slightly
different forms and eats the whole review budget. Without the second, a ``group''
of 5{,}000 members is not a lead --- it is a phone book.}

\subsection{XGBoost --- the measuring stick}

Described in Section~\ref{sec:isitdl}. We run four measuring sticks: hand-written
rules; XGBoost on plain data; XGBoost plus neighbour information; and the
classical bid-rigging warning signs on their own.

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Why we deliberately built the thing most likely to beat us.} A 2023
published study (GADBench) found that tree methods often beat graph networks. Any
examiner who knows that study will ask whether we compared against it. We did.
It wins on one of the seven jobs, and Section~\ref{sec:isitdl} lays out exactly
which one and why.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{PGExplainer --- explaining the decision}

\pw{Once the model flags a group, we ask: which few lines actually caused this?
The explainer learns to keep the important lines and hide the rest, leaving a
small picture a human can read.}

\textbf{Why this one and not GNNExplainer?} We measured both. The older one works
out each explanation from scratch (slow) and produced explanations that failed a
basic sanity check on \textbf{41 out of 50} alerts. PGExplainer learns once, then
explains everything cheaply; failures dropped to \textbf{1 out of 50}, and it ran
2.4 times faster. We swapped on evidence.

\subsection{``Why that exact number?'' --- the settings, one by one}
\label{sec:whynumbers}

Every specific value in a system is a decision, and an examiner will pick one at
random and ask where it came from. Here is every one that matters.

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Start with the uncomfortable one, because it governs all the others.}

\textbf{We ran no hyperparameter search. None. On either side.}

Not for our models and not for the XGBoost measuring stick. Values below are
either standard published defaults or forced by the data.

Why admit that? Because the alternative is worse.

If we had tuned our models but not the baseline, every comparison would be
rigged in our favour and therefore worthless. Tuning both properly was beyond
the computing time we had. Tuning neither is the only remaining honest option.

And note which way it cuts. \textbf{The baseline wins every comparison it
enters.} So leaving it untuned works \emph{against} us, not for us. If we ever
do tune both, the most likely result is that our reported gap gets wider, not
narrower.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{3.5cm}p{1.6cm}p{9.9cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Setting} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Why that value} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Message-passing rounds (layers) & \textbf{2} &
Each round lets a dot see one step further. Two rounds means a dot is described
by its neighbours and its neighbours' neighbours --- which is the scale our nine
shapes live at. Going deeper is not just slower: with more rounds every dot's
description drifts towards the average of the whole network, a known failure
called over-smoothing, and the thing that makes a group distinctive washes out. \\
\addlinespace
Hidden size & \textbf{64} &
The standard default in this literature. Big enough to hold a useful summary,
small enough to train on a laptop. Not searched. \\
\addlinespace
Attention heads & \textbf{4} &
Lets the model attend to several kinds of neighbour at once (say, one head for
large amounts and one for timing) rather than being forced into a single view.
Four is the common default. Not searched. \\
\addlinespace
Dropout & \textbf{0.3} &
Randomly ignores 30\% of the signal during training so the model cannot lean on
any single feature. Standard value. \\
\addlinespace
Focal loss strength ($\gamma$) & \textbf{2.0} &
The value published with the method. $\gamma=0$ would make it ordinary loss;
higher values concentrate harder on difficult rare cases. We use the published
default and \emph{report} the comparison against weighted cross-entropy rather
than tuning to win it. \\
\addlinespace
Review budgets & \textbf{50 / 100 / 200} &
Taken from the problem statement's own operational framing (``top 100''), with
one lower and one higher so the trend is visible rather than a single
cherry-picked point. Procurement additionally uses top 1 / 5 / 10\% because
tender queues differ hugely in length between countries. \\
\addlinespace
Duplicate-removal threshold & \textbf{0.5} &
Two alerts sharing more than half their members are the same lead twice. Below
0.5 you would start merging genuinely different groups that happen to share a
few entities. \textbf{And we did not simply assert this} --- the sensitivity
sweep varies it, and the queue keeps all 254 alerts at every threshold tested,
so this choice provably does not drive any published number. \\
\addlinespace
Alert size cap & \textbf{100 members} &
A ``group'' of thousands is not a lead, it is a phone book --- and without a cap
one mega-cluster absorbs the whole review budget while being impossible to
investigate. 100 is roughly the largest group a human team can still work
through. \\
\addlinespace
Hit rule & \textbf{$\geq 1$ confirmed} &
The lenient choice, chosen deliberately \emph{and then tested against stricter
ones} (10\% and 25\% of members confirmed). Results held. Had we only reported
the lenient rule, that would be a fair criticism; reporting it alongside the
strict ones is the answer to it. \\
\addlinespace
Seeds per campaign & \textbf{5} &
Enough to expose instability --- and it did: it revealed our best single result
(0.5492) was a lucky run against a true average of 0.4729. More would tighten
the estimate, but each seed is a full training run, and five already changed
our conclusions. Below five you cannot see a spread at all. \\
\addlinespace
Explanation neighbourhood & \textbf{2 hops} &
Matched to the model: a 2-layer network can only ever use information from 2
hops away, so explaining over a wider region would be showing evidence the model
never actually saw. \\
\addlinespace
Explanations produced & \textbf{queue head} &
Explanations cost real computation per alert. They are generated for the top of
each queue, which is what a team at a fixed budget will actually open. Lower
alerts still carry a score and a network. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

An examiner will ask, so here are the honest answers.

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{3.9cm}p{11.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Not used} & \textbf{Why not} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{The original GAT} & GATv2 fixes a proven flaw at identical cost. Kept

\textbf{Louvain} & Can produce groups whose members are not connected to each

\section{How We Measure Success}
\label{sec:metrics}

\subsection{The four outcomes: TP, TN, FP, FN}
\label{sec:confusion}

Every measurement in this report is built from four possible outcomes. They are
worth ten minutes because every other number is made of them.

\pw{You point at some groups and say ``check these''. Reality then has its own
opinion. There are exactly four ways your call and reality can line up.}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.3cm}p{6.3cm}p{6.3cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{We flagged it} & \textbf{We did not flag it} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Really was bad} &
\textbf{True Positive (TP)} \newline
We said check it; it was genuinely a cartel or laundering ring. \newline
\emph{The win.} &
\textbf{False Negative (FN)} \newline
A real ring existed and we stayed silent. \newline
\emph{The dangerous miss --- the crime goes unexamined.} \\
\addlinespace
\textbf{Really was fine} &
\textbf{False Positive (FP)} \newline
We sent an investigator to look at something innocent. \newline
\emph{The expensive mistake --- wasted work, and unfair attention on an innocent
firm.} &
\textbf{True Negative (TN)} \newline
Ordinary activity we correctly left alone. \newline
\emph{The silent success --- and there is an enormous amount of it.} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection*{How the metrics are built from those four}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Measure} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{The question it answers} \\
\midrule
Precision & TP / (TP + FP) &
Of the cases we sent you, how many were real? \\
Recall & TP / (TP + FN) &
Of all the real cases out there, how many did we catch? \\
False-positive rate & FP / (FP + TN) &
Of everything innocent, what share did we bother? \\
Accuracy & (TP + TN) / everything &
\emph{Useless here} --- see below. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Precision and recall pull against each other, and that is the whole
job.}

Flag everything and you catch every ring (recall 1.0) but almost all your work
is wasted (precision near zero). Flag nothing and you never waste a minute
(no false positives) but you catch nothing.

A screening tool lives on that trade-off, and the review budget is where you
choose to sit on it. That is why we never quote a single quality number without
saying at what budget.
\end{keybox}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{A subtlety this project cannot ignore: ``unconfirmed'' is not
``innocent''.}

On the Bitcoin data about 76\% of items carry \emph{no answer at all}. So when
we call something a false positive, what we honestly mean is ``we flagged it and
could not confirm it''. It may well be a true positive nobody has yet verified.

This cuts one way only: it means our reported precision is a
\textbf{lower bound}, and our real-world false-positive count is probably lower
than the number we publish. We do not quietly claim that benefit --- we report
the pessimistic figure and state the caveat, and the Model Lab prints
``unconfirmed $\neq$ false'' next to the queue chart for exactly this reason.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Why we never report ``accuracy''}

\begin{plain}
Take 100 payments. 2 are criminal, 98 are fine.

Now here is a model: it says ``everything is fine'' about all 100.

It is \textbf{98\% accurate}. It also caught zero criminals and is completely
useless. That is why accuracy is banned from this project's headlines.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Why we do not headline ROC-AUC either}

This is the single most common question, so here it is slowly.

\begin{plain}
Same 100 payments, 2 criminal.

A model puts the 2 criminals at positions 50 and 51 in its ranked list. You would
have to read 50 cases before finding your first criminal. In practice that is a
poor result.

But \textbf{ROC-AUC would still score this well}. The reason: ROC-AUC largely
rewards a model for correctly sorting the 98 innocent payments, and sorting the
easy majority is\ldots easy. With only 2 criminals in the pile, the score is
dominated by the part of the job that does not matter.
\end{plain}

\subsection{What we use instead: AUC-PR, always beside the guessing level}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{AUC-PR} refuses to give credit for sorting the innocent majority. It only
asks one thing:

\begin{center}
\emph{Of the cases you pointed at, how many were actually criminal?}
\end{center}

\ldots and it asks that repeatedly, at every possible list length, then averages
the answers.

\textbf{The number to compare it against.} If you shut your eyes and picked at
random, and 2 in 100 are criminal, you would score \textbf{0.02}. That is the
guessing level, and it is simply the fraction of criminals in the data.

So we print the guessing level next to every single score. On Elliptic the
guessing level is 0.065. Our deep model scores 0.4729.
\[
0.4729 \div 0.065 \approx 7.3
\]
\textbf{About 7 times better than guessing.} That sentence is what the number
means, and it is the sentence we want people to be able to say.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Precision@k --- the number that matters operationally}

\begin{plain}
This one needs no maths at all.

\emph{Your team can review 100 cases this week. We hand you our top 100. How many
of them turn out to be real?}

If the answer is 81, then Precision@100 is 0.81. That is it. It is the number an
actual investigator cares about, so it is the number we lead with.
\end{plain}

\subsection{What counts as a correct alert}

An alert is counted correct if it contains \textbf{at least one} confirmed
criminal or cartel member.

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{The obvious objection --- and our answer.}

A rule that lenient rewards a model for flagging enormous groups. Flag 5{,}000
firms and you are bound to include a guilty one.

That is exactly why we also (a) refuse alerts bigger than 100 members, (b) delete
near-duplicates, and (c) re-ran everything with much stricter rules, requiring
10\% and then 25\% of the group to be confirmed. The results held up under the
stricter rules.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{The rest of what we report}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Recall} --- of all the criminals that exist, how many did we catch?
  \item \textbf{False positive rate} --- how much innocent work did we create?
  \item \textbf{Per-time-step scores} --- so one good year cannot hide a collapse
        in another.
  \item \textbf{Five attempts, average and spread} --- a single lucky attempt is
        not a result.
  \item \textbf{A fairness test} (paired bootstrap) --- to check a gap is real and
        not luck.
  \item \textbf{Explanation fidelity} --- the system points at a few specific
        connections and says the decision depended on them. Delete exactly
        those, re-run the case, and see whether the warning really does
        disappear.
\end{itemize}

\section{What We Tried, and How the Results Changed}
\label{sec:tuning}

This is the full history in order, including the parts where we made ourselves
look worse. Those are the most instructive.

\subsection{Step 1 --- build the simple methods first}

Before any deep learning, we built the simple methods, so we would know what
``good'' even meant.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Elliptic (guessing level 0.065)} & \textbf{AUC-PR} & \textbf{Precision@100} \\
\midrule
Hand-written rules & 0.0576 & 0.00 \\
XGBoost & 0.8076 & 1.00 \\
XGBoost + neighbour info & \textbf{0.8104} & 1.00 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What we learned immediately:} the hand-written rules score \emph{below}
the guessing level of 0.065. They are worse than picking at random. This is the
classic complaint about rule-based fraud systems --- too many false alarms --- and
here it is, measured on real data.

\subsection{Step 2 --- the first deep models}

GATv2 with focal loss reached 0.693, with Precision@100 of 0.99. It looked like a
clear success.

\subsection{Step 3 --- the review that cut our own numbers}

We ran a deep internal review and found \textbf{30 defects}. One mattered
enormously.

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{The bug: the model was peeking at the test data.}

When scoring the test period, the code was re-computing its rescaling amounts
\emph{using the test data itself}. That is a subtle leak. The model was quietly
adjusting to the very change it was supposed to be measured against.

\textbf{The fix:} work out the rescaling on training data only, then lock it.

\textbf{The effect: our score fell from 0.693 to 0.532.}

We cut our own headline result by almost a quarter, because the higher number was
not honest. Deliberately letting a model adjust to new conditions is a legitimate
technique. Doing it \emph{by accident} and then reporting the result is not.
\end{warnbox}

We also found that tied scores were being handled in a way that flattered the
simple baselines. Every published number was regenerated after the fixes, and 34
new automatic tests were added so these specific bugs cannot come back.

\subsection{Step 4 --- learning that you must translate before combining}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{How we combined the models (5 attempts)} & \textbf{AUC-PR} \\
\midrule
Just averaging the rankings & 0.0511 $\pm$ 0.0019 \\
\textbf{Translating to real probabilities first} & \textbf{0.4434 $\pm$ 0.0501} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\pw{Averaging raw scores destroys the result, because the weak members shout over
the strong one. Translating first fixes it.} The fairness test says this gap is
real, not luck.

\subsection{Step 5 --- running it five times instead of once}

Running five attempts instead of one changed the story again. The 0.5492 we had
been quoting turned out to be \emph{the luckiest attempt}, not a typical one.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{One attempt} & \textbf{Average of 5, with spread} \\
\midrule
GATv2 with focal loss & 0.5492 & \textbf{0.4729 $\pm$ 0.0525} \\
GATv2 with weighted CE & 0.4869 & 0.4388 $\pm$ 0.0505 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What this changed:} the choice between the two loss functions had looked
settled after one attempt. Across five, the two overlap. So we now report that
choice as \emph{unresolved} rather than claiming a win we cannot support.

\subsection{Step 6 --- replacing the explainer}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{GNNExplainer} & \textbf{PGExplainer} \\
\midrule
Passes the basic sanity check & 12 out of 50 & \textbf{49 out of 50} \\
Produces a nonsensical score & 41 out of 50 & \textbf{1 out of 50} \\
Speed & baseline & \textbf{2.4$\times$ faster} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Step 7 --- removing parts to see what each was worth}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{What we changed} & \textbf{Effect on the score} & \textbf{Conclusion} \\
\midrule
Took away edge directions & \textbf{$-$0.194} & The most important ingredient \\
Took away the no-labels models & $+$0.030 & Removing them actually helps \\
Took away focal loss & $-$0.034 & Too small to call \\
Added context-fusion gating & $-$0.225 & Rejected \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{How to read this table.} The middle column is the effect \emph{of making

The two kinds of row are not comparable with each other. Only ``took away'' rows
can tell you which ingredient matters most.
\end{plain}

\textbf{About the no-labels models.} Taking them out \emph{improves} the combined
score by 0.030. We keep them anyway, because they are the only thing that works
when there is no answer key --- which is the entire 163{,}000-node study and any
realistic deployment. We keep them for coverage, not for the headline, and we say
so rather than quietly dropping a part that does not flatter us.

\subsection{Step 8 --- the dashboard, four attempts}

The dashboard was rejected three times before it passed, and then once more on
colour.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{V1} --- rejected: flat and all one colour.
  \item \textbf{V2} --- added five colours, visible glass panels and hover effects.
        Still rejected: too quiet.
  \item \textbf{V3} --- added real graphics-chip rendering and proper tabs.
        Accepted.
  \item \textbf{V4} --- answered a final complaint: ``still too blue''.
\end{itemize}

For V4 we measured the problem instead of guessing. The old background was
painting \textbf{59.1\% of the screen's pixels a strong blue}. After the fix it is
\textbf{0\%}, and the average background colour moved from RGB(21,25,40) to
RGB(11,12,15) --- effectively black. Colour is now used only where it \emph{means}
something: risk level, category, which tab you are on.

XGBoost + neighbour info & 0.3775 & 0.56 \\
Warning-sign features only & 0.3811 & 0.78 \\
\textbf{XGBoost + warning signs} & \textbf{0.4558} & 0.61 \\
R-GCN (5 attempts) & 0.2808 $\pm$ 0.0087 & --- \\

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{An honest failure.} Our graph model scores \textbf{below the guessing
level} here --- 0.2808 against 0.358 --- and it does so consistently across all
five attempts. Consistency means this is a real weakness, not bad luck.

Two reasons: the world changed between the training years and the testing years,
and award-only data simply carries a weak signal. On this dataset the best result
comes from the spreadsheet-style method.

We did not delete this arm. A clear negative result from a fair test is a
finding.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Does it work somewhere new?}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{García, leaving one market out:} works on \emph{every} market
        tested. On average 1.57 times better than guessing. In Italy, the top 10
        picks were all correct.
  \item \textbf{Mendeley, leaving one country out:} depends on the country. On
        average 1.17 times better than guessing --- but \textbf{the largest country
        fails}, at 0.90, which is worse than guessing there. An average that hides
        a failure is a misleading average, so we publish every country.
  \item \textbf{Between crime types:} contracts to money is weakly positive (0.1501
        against a guessing level of 0.065). Money to contracts is negative.
  \item \textbf{When labels are scarce:} transferring \textbf{never} helps below
        500 labelled examples, in either direction. A clean negative answer to a
        question nobody had tested before.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The big planted-cartel study --- the clearest deep learning win}

We planted 100 fake cartels (940 member firms) into the 163{,}327-dot Georgian
contract network and measured how many we recovered, five times over, with
\textbf{no answer key available to the detectors}.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{What this tells a deployment team.} Reviewing just 1.2\% of the network,

\pw{If the cheats leave a visible web of connections between them, we find them.
If they simply agree to take turns, and never appear together anywhere, then
shape alone cannot see it --- you would need prices and dates.}

Knowing precisely which cartels this tool will and will not catch is far more
useful than a single average score.

\textbf{And note the method:} the deep autoencoder scores 0.9137 here, while the
non-learning floor scores 0.1237. XGBoost cannot take part at all, because there
is no answer key. This is deep learning doing something nothing else can.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Robustness}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Results do not change when we vary the duplicate-removal threshold or
        tighten the ``what counts as correct'' rule. The protocol is not doing the
        work for us.
  \item The Georgian data processing and the tree results reproduce
        \textbf{exactly, byte for byte, on three different computers}.
  \item \textbf{An important warning.} The validation score sits at 0.93--0.95
        while the real test score sits at 0.42--0.55. Worse, deliberately
        corrupting up to 20\% of the training answers \emph{raises} the test score
        to 0.5978 while the validation score collapses. So under changing
        conditions, our validation set is not a trustworthy guide. We report this
        rather than quietly using validation to pick winners.
\end{itemize}

The page has four parts: the system flowchart, the method cards, the cloud

\textbf{If you remember one sentence about this architecture, make it this

The bottom half can only \emph{read} them.

\subsection{Stages 1 and 2 --- getting two different worlds into one shape}

\textbf{What it is.} Six readers, one per dataset, each checking the file's
fingerprint before it touches anything. Then a converter that turns whatever
came out of those readers into a single common format: things, connections
between things, and --- where they exist --- answers. The time-splitting rules
are applied in the same box.

\textbf{Why it exists.} Because otherwise this project would be two projects. A
bank ledger and a public-tender register have nothing in common on the surface:
different columns, different identifiers, different time units, different words
for everything. Box 2 is where that surface difference is deliberately thrown
away. After it, the system genuinely does not know which crime it is looking
at. A bank account and a construction firm arrive as the same kind of object
with the same kind of connections.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Two things, and the second is worse than the
first. First, every stage downstream would have to be written twice, tested
twice, and audited twice --- two sets of bugs, two sets of leakage risks.
Second, and fatally for the research question: \textbf{the cross-domain
experiments would be meaningless}. Asking ``does knowledge of money laundering
transfer to bid rigging?'' only makes sense if both are expressed in the same
language. The shared format is what makes that question askable at all.

\textbf{The checksum is not bureaucracy either.} If a download is truncated or
somebody swaps a file, the reader stops. Without it the pipeline would train
happily on the wrong data and produce numbers that look completely normal.

\aloud{Everything gets converted into one common format --- things,
connections, and where we have them, answers. After that box the system doesn't
know which crime it's looking at. That's the trick of the whole project.}

\subsection{Stage 3 --- four opinions, formed at the same time}

\textbf{What it is.} Four boxes side by side, because they genuinely run in
parallel over the same network, each producing its own score for every dot.
\textbf{3a} is three deep models that learn from labelled examples. One works
out which of a thing's connections matter most. One treats money in differently
from money out. One handles several relationship types separately.
\textbf{3b}, two models that need no labels at all: they learn what
normal looks like and flag whatever they cannot rebuild. \textbf{3c},
hand-computed measurements --- how many connections, which shapes a thing sits
inside, and for contracts the classic warning signs. \textbf{3d}, the honesty
group: the strongest non-deep method we could field, the planted-cartel
studies, and the automated test suite.

\textbf{Why four and not one.} Each arm fails in a different way, and the ways
do not overlap. The supervised models are strong where labels exist and useless
where they do not. The unlabelled models work anywhere but are noisy. The
hand-computed features are interpretable and shallow. The baselines exist so
that no deep-learning claim is made against a straw man.

\textbf{What breaks without arm 3b.} This is the one people ask to delete,
because it has the worst headline score. Delete it and the project loses its
clearest result. In the Georgian planted-cartel study there is \emph{no answer
key at all}, so the supervised models and XGBoost cannot participate --- and the
unlabelled model recovers about nine in ten of the bid-together cartels while
the non-learning floor manages about one in eight. The arm with the worst score
on the labelled data is the only arm that works in the deployment case that
actually matters.

\textbf{What breaks without arm 3d.} Credibility. A deep-learning result with
no strong classical comparison is not a result, it is a demonstration. Arm 3d is
also why this report can say plainly that on Elliptic the trees win.

\aloud{Four different opinions about the same accounts, formed at the same time.
One group learns from known answers. One needs no answers at all. One is
hand-computed measurements. And the fourth is there so nobody can accuse us of
only racing against ourselves.}

\subsection{Stage 4 --- combining the four opinions}

\textbf{What it is.} One box that turns four scores per thing into one, with
one crucial ordering constraint: each model's output is first translated onto a
proper probability scale, checked against held-out data, and only then merged.

\textbf{Why the ordering matters more than the merging.} Two models can both be
excellent at ranking and still be on completely different scales. One is
confident at 0.9; the other is certain at 0.1. Averaging those raw lets the loud
one flatten the quiet one regardless of which was right.

\textbf{What breaks without it --- measured, not asserted.} We ran it both ways
over five attempts. Translate first and the combined result is
\textbf{0.4434}; skip the translation and it collapses to \textbf{0.0511},
which is essentially the guessing level of 0.065. Same models, same data, same
seeds. That single ordering decision was worth more than any model we trained.

\aloud{Two models can both be good at ranking and still be on totally different
scales. Combine them raw and the loud one flattens the quiet one. So we put
them on the same scale first --- and we measured what happens if you don't. It
collapses to roughly what guessing gets you.}

\subsection{Stage 5 --- turning scores into somebody's morning}

\textbf{What it is.} Communities are found in the test-window network. Each
community inherits a score from its members. Near-duplicates are removed and
oversized groups excluded. The list is then cut at the number of cases the team
can review. Output: 254 alerts on the money side, 223 on the contracts side.

\textbf{Why it exists.} Up to here the system has a score on all 67{,}504 test
things. That is a research artefact, not a product. Nobody investigates
67{,}504 items, and a ranked list of individual accounts is barely better,
because collusion is not something one account does. It takes at least two.
The unit of investigation has to be the group.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Two failures. Without the grouping you would
hand an analyst a list of individuals and leave them to reconstruct the ring by
hand --- which is the work you were trying to save. Without the duplicate
removal the same ring appears three times under three reference numbers, and
your measured precision silently counts it three times.

\textbf{Note what this stage does not use: the answers.} Grouping is structural
only. That is deliberate, and it is why the identical grouping step runs on the
Georgian corpus, where no cartel labels exist.

\aloud{We've got a score on sixty-seven thousand accounts, which helps nobody.
So we find the clusters, rank them, throw away near-duplicates, and cut the
list at whatever the team can review. Two hundred and fifty-four cases. That's
a morning's work, not a research output.}

\subsection{Stage 6 --- why an alert is not enough on its own}

\textbf{What it is.} Three things happen to every case at the head of the
queue. The explainer isolates the few connections the decision actually
depended on. A set of hand-written rules checks the case against nine known
shapes and names one only when it can formally prove it. And where a shape
matches, the official warning sign it corresponds to is attached, quoted, with
its source.

\textbf{Why it exists.} An unexplained alert is not actionable and, in this
domain, not defensible. Somebody has to be able to say \emph{why} a firm was
looked at, to that firm's lawyer if necessary.

\textbf{Why the three parts are deliberately independent.} The explainer is
learned and can be wrong. The matcher is deterministic and cannot invent a
shape. The warning-sign citation is quoted text from a fixed reference file.
An investigator who distrusts the neural network entirely can throw away the
first part and the other two still stand on their own --- and that is the
property that makes this usable rather than merely impressive.

\textbf{What breaks without the verification step.} Explanations that sound
good and are not true. So each one is tested twice. The explainer has pointed
at a few specific connections --- say three accounts and the four payments
between them, out of a group of eighty-five. Test one: show the model
\emph{only} those four payments and hide everything else; does the same alert
still come out? Test two: put the whole group back but \emph{delete} those four
payments; does the alert disappear? Before we replaced the explainer, 38 of our 50 published
Bitcoin explanations failed that check. Now one does. We kept the old figure in
writing rather than quietly improving it.

\aloud{The explainer says which few connections mattered --- and then we check
it, by re-running the case with only those connections, and again with them
taken away. And separately, hand-written rules name the shape, but only when
they can prove it.}

\subsection{Stage 7 --- why the results are just files}

\textbf{What it is.} Everything the pipeline produced goes into ordinary files
in a folder: the alert table, one file per explanation, the metrics, and a
small index telling the website what exists.

\textbf{Why it exists.} It sounds unambitious and it is one of the better
decisions in the project. Files can be copied, fingerprinted, versioned,
handed to an examiner on a memory stick, and rolled back by pointing at a
different folder. A database would have given us nothing here and taken away
all of that.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} The trust boundary, which is the next
subsection. A file opened read-only cannot be modified by the thing reading it.
A running database service shared between the trainer and the website could be.

\aloud{Everything goes into ordinary files in a folder. Not a database. Files
can be copied, checksummed, versioned, emailed to an examiner --- and rolled
back by pointing at a different folder.}

\subsection{The trust boundary --- the most important line on the page}

\textbf{What it is.} A single horizontal line across the diagram. Above it, the
pipeline: it trains, it scores, it writes files, and then the process exits.
Below it, the product: the doorway, the assistant, the website. The only thing
crossing the line is files being read.

\textbf{Why it exists.} The two halves have opposite risk profiles. Training is
powerful --- it can change what the system believes about a person. The website
is the part the outside world can reach, so it is the part that will be
attacked. Those two properties must never live in the same process.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Imagine somebody breaks into the public
website tomorrow. With the boundary, they can read published results and
nothing else: they cannot retrain a model, cannot change anybody's score, and
cannot plant evidence in a case file. Without it, the most damaging attack on
this system is not stealing data --- it is \emph{quietly altering who looks
suspicious}, which is far harder to notice and far worse in its consequences.

\textbf{And it pays for itself twice.} Because the serving half never trains
anything, it needs no expensive graphics hardware. It starts instantly, runs on
the cheapest machine on the price list, and demonstrates on a laptop with the
wifi switched off. The security argument and the cost argument are the same
argument.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The strongest form of this claim.} In the cloud design
(Section~\ref{sec:cloud}) the boundary is not enforced by our own code being
polite. The serving side is granted \emph{read-only} permissions on the
results storage by the cloud provider itself. Our code could contain a bug that
tries to write, and it would still be refused.
\end{keybox}

\aloud{Above the line we train, and then we stop. Below the line we show people
things, and we can't change anything. If somebody breaks into our website
tomorrow they can read results --- they cannot retrain a model or change
anyone's score.}

\subsection{Stages 8 to 10 --- the doorway, the assistant, and the person}

\textbf{The doorway (box 8).} A small read-only service that hands out the
stored files. Ask for a case, it gives you the case. Ask for the network, it
trims it to the alert's own members plus one hop of context, on the server,
before sending. Without that trimming the browser would receive a
million-line network, and would either freeze or --- worse --- show a silently
cropped slice that the reader assumes is the whole thing. Every response
carries the ethics wording, not just the pretty pages.

\textbf{The assistant, and why it sits beside the doorway rather than behind
it.} On the diagram the Copilot is a separate box with both arrows pointing in
and out of the same read-only service. That placement is the point: it has no
private access to anything. It reads exactly what a human reading the dashboard
could read. Every number it states must appear literally in what it looked up;
accusing language is rewritten on the way out; and it sits a 24-question exam
before every release. \textbf{Without those guards} you have a confident
narrator attached to a screening system, which is the single most dangerous
component you could add to a project like this.

\textbf{The person (box 10).} A human is drawn on the architecture diagram on
purpose. The system produces a ranked list and the evidence behind it; a person
decides what happens next, within a fixed number of cases. That is not modesty
about model quality. \textbf{If a screening tool made the escalation decision,
you would have automated an accusation.}

\aloud{The doorway is read-only --- there's no write endpoint to abuse, because
none exists. The assistant sits beside it, not behind it, so it can only read
what you could read. And at the bottom of the diagram there's a person, on
purpose.}

\subsection{The method cards}

Below the flowchart the page has twelve explanation cards, each with a ``what
it does'' and a ``how it works'' line: graph networks, attention, GraphSAGE and
R-GCN, focal loss, the autoencoders, combining scores, grouping and duplicate

\section{The Cloud Design}
\label{sec:cloud}

The second diagram shows how this would run as a real hosted product. It has
\textbf{two halves}, and keeping them apart is the entire point --- it is the
same trust boundary as Section~\ref{sec:arch}, redrawn in cloud services.

The service names below are Amazon's. They matter less than what each one is
\emph{for}, so each is explained the same way as the pipeline stages: what it
is, why it exists, what breaks without it.

\subsection{Half 1 --- the part that is always on (cheap, no graphics chip)}

\subsubsection*{How a request reaches the dashboard at all}

An analyst opens a browser and types our address. Three things then happen
before anything of ours runs.

\textbf{Route 53} is the internet's address book: it turns our name into a real
machine's location. \textbf{CloudFront} keeps copies of the dashboard in data
centres around the world, so the page loads from somewhere near the analyst
rather than from one machine on one continent. \textbf{WAF} sits in front of
everything and blocks the well-known attack patterns before they reach us. And
the whole conversation runs over \textbf{HTTPS}, so nobody in between can read
it or alter it in flight.

\textbf{What breaks without them.} Drop Route 53 and users type an IP address.
Drop CloudFront and the site is slow for anybody far away, and every visitor
hits our one small machine directly --- so the cheapest tier stops being viable
the moment more than a handful of people use it. Drop WAF and the first
automated scanner that finds us starts probing the doorway itself. Drop HTTPS
and alert data --- which is data about who is under suspicion --- crosses the
internet in the clear.

\aloud{Route 53 turns our web address into a machine. CloudFront keeps copies
of the site around the world so it loads fast wherever you are. And there's a
filter in front of everything that blocks the common attacks.}

\subsubsection*{Where the dashboard itself lives}

\textbf{S3, the website bucket.} The dashboard is a folder of files --- there is
no server rendering pages. Those files sit in cheap storage, in a private area
that only CloudFront is allowed to read.

\textbf{Why private rather than a public bucket.} A publicly readable bucket is
one misconfiguration away from being writable by somebody else, and it bypasses
the protective layer in front. Making the delivery network the only reader
means every visitor goes through the filter, without exception.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Nothing about the machine learning. This is
purely how you serve a static site cheaply and safely, and it is why hosting
the dashboard costs cents rather than dollars.

\subsubsection*{Where the compute is, and why it starts as Lambda}

\textbf{The compute} runs the doorway and the assistant. At launch it is
\textbf{Lambda}: code that runs on demand and costs nothing while nobody is
using it. As usage grows it becomes \textbf{containers across two data
centres} behind a load balancer.

\textbf{Why start small.} The honest state of a university project is that
nobody is using it most of the time. Paying by the hour for an idle machine is
paying for nothing. Lambda's pricing shape matches the actual usage shape.

\textbf{Why the migration is not a rewrite.} The service is stateless and
read-only. It holds no session, writes nothing, and can be started and stopped
freely --- so moving it to containers is a configuration change. That property
came free from the trust boundary; it was not designed for separately.

\textbf{What breaks without the two data centres at tier 3.} Nothing until a
data centre has a bad day, at which point everything. That is why it is a tier-3
line item and not a tier-1 one: you buy it when the tool is load-bearing.

\aloud{At launch the doorway is code that only runs when somebody asks for
something, so it costs nothing while idle. When it gets busy it becomes proper
containers in two data centres --- and that's a config change, not a rewrite.}

\subsubsection*{Where the results live, and why versioning is the safety net}

\textbf{S3, the results bucket.} Every pipeline run writes into its own dated
folder --- \code{runs/<run\_id>/} --- and nothing is ever overwritten in place.
The website is pointed at one folder.

\textbf{Why this is the most operationally important box in the diagram.}
Models get worse sometimes. A retrain lands, the queue looks wrong, and
somebody has to decide what to do at four in the afternoon. Here that decision
is: point at yesterday's folder. Seconds, no retraining, no panic, and the bad
run is still on disk to be investigated rather than destroyed.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Rolling back means retraining, which means
hours of graphics-machine time and a period where the tool is either wrong or
down. And a half-finished run --- a job killed mid-write --- could corrupt the
good results it was overwriting. With per-run folders it simply produces an
incomplete folder nobody is pointing at.

\aloud{Every run writes into its own dated folder. So undoing a bad model is
pointing at yesterday's folder --- no retraining, no panic --- and the bad run
is still there to look at.}

\subsubsection*{The pieces that exist so nothing goes quietly wrong}

\textbf{ECR} stores our container images and keeps the last three, so a
rollback of the \emph{code} is as cheap as a rollback of the results.

\textbf{Secrets Manager} holds the assistant's key. \emph{Why:} so the key is
never in the code, never in the image, and never in the repository. This project
has already had one key exposure in an inherited file, which is exactly the
failure this box prevents.

\textbf{IAM, read-only.} The serving side is granted read-only rights on the
results bucket. \textbf{This is the strongest sentence in the cloud design}: the
trust boundary is enforced by Amazon, not by our own code being well-behaved.
Our code could contain a bug that tries to write, and the write would still be
refused.

\textbf{CloudWatch} collects the logs and raises alarms. \emph{What breaks
without it:} you find out the pipeline has been failing for a week when a user
tells you the dates look old.

\textbf{The budget alarm} is switched on from day one. \emph{What breaks
without it:} a stuck job or a runaway assistant loop spends real money for days
before anybody notices. This is a student project; that is not a hypothetical
risk.

\aloud{The public side is given read-only rights to the results. So that
boundary I showed you isn't just our own code being polite --- Amazon enforces
it.}

\subsection{Half 2 --- the part that only exists while it runs}

\textbf{EventBridge} is an alarm clock: every night, or every week, it starts
the pipeline. If the job fails, an alert goes out.

\textbf{The spot GPU job} rents a graphics computer at roughly 70\% off the
normal price, then reads the data, trains, scores, groups, explains, writes the
results \textbf{and shuts itself down}.

\textbf{Why spot pricing is safe here, when it usually is not.} Spot machines
can be taken away with little warning, which is unacceptable for anything
serving live requests. This job serves nothing. If it is interrupted, the
website carries on showing yesterday's results, an alarm fires, and the job runs
again tonight. Nothing was overwritten in place, so a half-finished run cannot
corrupt a good one. The workload's tolerance for interruption is exactly what
earns the discount.

\textbf{The upward arrow} is the only connection between the two halves: the
nightly job \emph{writes} new results into the storage the website
\emph{reads}. There is no arrow coming back down, and that absence is the
design.

\textbf{What breaks if you merge the halves.} You would be paying for a
graphics machine around the clock in order to serve requests that need no
graphics machine at all --- and it is the largest line on the bill, so that is
where nearly all of the cost difference between the tiers comes from. You would
also have put the trainable component inside the process the internet can
reach, which is the exact thing Section~\ref{sec:arch} spends a page avoiding.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why this shape saves most of the money.}

Graphics chips are the expensive part. The obvious approach is to keep one
running all the time. But we only need one \emph{while training}, which is a few
hours a week. Showing results needs none at all.

So the expensive machine exists for two hours and then vanishes, while the
always-on part is a tiny cheap service. That is why the demo version costs about
\$10 a month, and effectively nothing once standard free student credits are
applied.
\end{keybox}

\aloud{There's an alarm clock. It rents a graphics computer at about seventy
percent off, trains, writes the results, and then shuts itself down. And follow
that arrow going up --- it's the only link between the two halves, and there's
nothing coming back down.}

\subsection{What it would cost}

\begin{center}\small

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{A correction we owe the reader.} An earlier version of this report said
AMLworld was blocked on ``credentials and graphics hardware we do not have''.
That was asserted, not checked, and it was wrong on both counts.

Checked on 2026-07-25: the credentials are present, the raw files are
downloaded (475\,MB), and the dataset is already converted into our shared
format with its full answer key --- 6{,}357 illicit accounts out of 515{,}088.
So we simply ran it.

\textbf{It trains on this ordinary laptop, on the normal processor, in 52
seconds.} No graphics card involved. The hardware claim was false.
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{What the run actually showed} (test set 242{,}427 accounts, blind
guessing $=0.0104$):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{AUC-PR} & \textbf{vs guessing} \\
\midrule
XGBoost & 0.0064 & \textbf{0.62$\times$} --- worse than guessing \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why it fails --- diagnosed, not guessed.}

Two things go wrong together.

\textbf{(1) The answer key is on the wrong thing.} AMLworld marks \emph{which
payments} launder, not which accounts. To get an account-level answer we mark an
account bad if \emph{any} of its payments was bad.

\pw{You are told which phone \emph{calls} were criminal and asked to find the
criminal \emph{people}. Someone who makes 5{,}000 calls a year, one of which was
criminal, looks exactly like everyone else.}

\textbf{(2) The test half has almost nothing to look at.} Splitting by when an
account first appears puts \emph{brand-new} accounts in the test half. We
measured what they look like:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Train half} & \textbf{Test half} \\
\midrule
Typical number of connections & 13 & \textbf{3} \\
\ \ …for the criminal accounts & 25 & 18 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A typical test account has \textbf{three} connections. There is barely any
network there to judge, so a method that works by looking at company kept has
almost nothing to work with --- and it lands below blind guessing.

\textbf{So the blocker was never credentials and never hardware. It is that we
are asking the wrong question of this dataset,} and a queue built from a model
at 0.60$\times$ guessing would mislead anyone who opened it. That is why it stays
off the queue screens --- and it cost 52 seconds to establish rather than being
asserted.
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection*{So what \emph{is} AMLworld used for? (And what it just told us)}

The plan always gave it a different job: \emph{``validating that the detector
recovers known injected patterns''}. That job needs no answer key, so neither
problem above applies --- and it is the one thing a 5-million-payment real
financial network is uniquely good for.

So we planted 100 laundering rings of known shape (640 accounts in total) into
the real network and measured how many we could find while reviewing only the
top 2{,}000 of 262{,}921 accounts --- that is 0.76\% of the network.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Planted shape} & \textbf{Simple scorer} & \textbf{Autoencoder A} &
\textbf{Autoencoder B} & \textbf{Combined} \\
\midrule
Hidden common owner & \textbf{0.5625} & 0.1000 & 0.0375 & 0.1375 \\
Money going in a circle & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 \\
Funnelling in & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 \\
Spraying out & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 \\
Pass-through chain & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 & 0.0000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{This is the same finding as the procurement side, on a completely
different dataset --- which is what makes it worth having.}

Only \textbf{hidden common ownership} is catchable by shape alone, and the
\emph{simplest possible scorer} catches it best (0.56, beating both deep
autoencoders). Accounts secretly sharing an owner form a tight cluster, and a
tight cluster is easy to spot.

The four \emph{flow} patterns --- circling, funnelling, spraying, passing
through --- \textbf{escape completely}. Every one scores zero.

\pw{If the criminals leave a visible web of connections between them, we find
them. If they merely move money along in a particular pattern, the shape of the
network alone cannot see it. You would need the amounts and the timings.}

We now have this result three times over: on Bitcoin, on Georgian contracts, and
now on 5 million synthetic bank payments. A finding that repeats on three
unrelated datasets is worth far more than a good score on one.
\end{keybox}

\textbf{And it tells a deployment team exactly what to expect.} This tool will
surface shell-company structures. It will not, on its own, surface layering.
That is a sentence worth more than a headline number, and it is why AMLworld
earns its place in the project even without appearing on the queue screens.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{The short version for a presentation:}

``Four of the six have a trained ranking model, so those four appear in the
console.

The fifth, the Georgian contract data, is deliberately unlabelled. It is where
we plant fake cartels and measure how many we find.

The sixth, AMLworld, we use for the same kind of test. It has no ranked queue
because its answer key marks payments rather than accounts, so account-level
scoring performs worse than guessing on it --- and we would rather show nothing
than show that.''
\end{plain}

Five things never leave the frame, and each is there for a reason worth stating.

\subsubsection*{The domain switch}

\textbf{What it is.} Two pills, top right, reading \textbf{Financial · AML} and
\textbf{Procurement · bid rigging}. Flipping it changes the colour scheme, the

\textbf{Why it exists.} It is the two-worlds thesis made operable in one

than two: the same reader, the same scoring, the same grouping, the same
evidence rules serve both sides.

\textbf{Why the crime is named on the pill.} Until 2026-07-25 the pills said
only ``Financial'' and ``Procurement''. An audit then found the letters AML
appeared nowhere on the console except two buried lines in the About page --- so
a visitor could not tell what the financial side was screening \emph{for}. Naming

either pill gives a one-sentence definition.

\aloud{This switch is the whole two-worlds idea in one control. Watch the
colours, the datasets and the alerts change --- and nothing underneath change at
all.}

\subsubsection*{The dataset picker}

\textbf{What it is.} Two datasets per side, four in total, each a real public
dataset with a finished scored run behind it.

\textbf{Why two per side rather than one.} Because one dataset per domain
invites the reasonable suspicion that the result is a property of that dataset.
On the financial side the two are the \emph{same} Bitcoin world at different
zoom levels --- individual payments, and wallets --- which is a genuine test of
whether the approach survives a change of granularity.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Nothing technically. What you lose is the
answer to ``does this work anywhere else?'' at demonstration time, and that is
the second question every evaluator asks.

\subsubsection*{The six tabs}

\textbf{What they are.} Overview, Alert Queue, Graph Explorer, Case Detail,
Model Lab, About --- each with a fixed colour and icon that never change.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{The bar is one row, and it stays one row.} An earlier fix for a 16-pixel
overflow simply let the header wrap, which pushed the dataset picker, the domain
switch and the assistant button onto a second line. The result was a tall bar
holding small controls with a large empty gap beside them --- it read as a
layout accident rather than a design, and it was reported as such.

It now stays on a single line at every width, and the two widest optional pieces
yield in order as the window narrows: the ``Integrity Screening Console'' tag
line disappears below 1536 pixels, and the tab \emph{labels} disappear below
1280, leaving the six icons (each keeps a tooltip). Measured at 1100 pixels: one
row, no overflow, controls ending 24 pixels inside the edge. The controls
themselves were also sized up to match the bar they sit in --- everything in the
header is now one consistent 13-pixel type size.
\end{plain}

\textbf{Why fixed colours.} They are a navigation aid, not decoration: after a
minute of use you locate a screen by its colour before you read its name. The
one colour deliberately \emph{not} used for any tab is coral, which means
``flagged'' and nothing else anywhere in the product.

\subsubsection*{The ethics line in the footer}

\textbf{What it is.} A single fixed sentence, on every screen, stating that the
system produces screening signals and not determinations of wrongdoing.

\textbf{Why it is in the footer and not in a dialog.} A dialog is dismissed once
and then forgotten. This has to be visible at the moment somebody is looking at
a named entity with a high score, because that is the moment the mistake gets
made.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} Nothing visible, and that is the danger. A
screening tool whose caveat lives only in its documentation will eventually be
quoted without it. The same sentence is attached to every API response too, so
it survives being read by a machine.

\subsubsection*{The assistant}

Openable from any screen, and covered in its own subsection below.

\subsection{Screen 1 --- Overview}

\textbf{What the screen is for.} It answers, in one glance, the four questions a
manager asks before any of the detail matters. How much came out. How much of
it is urgent. How much work have we signed up for. And how much data is behind
it.

\subsubsection*{The four tiles}

\textbf{Alerts in queue} is how many suspicious groups this dataset produced ---
254 on the Bitcoin payments data, 223 on the European contracts data.
\textbf{High-band alerts} is how many of those sit in the top risk band.
\textbf{Cases to review} is the number of cases the team has said it can get
through. \textbf{Datasets} is how many are loaded on this side of the domain
switch.

\textbf{Why the third tile is the important one.} Everything else on the console
is measured \emph{at} that number. A screening system without a stated workload
has no meaningful accuracy at all, because you can catch everything if you are
willing to review everything. Putting the workload on the opening screen, next
to the alert count, is the argument for Precision@k as this project's headline
metric rather than accuracy or ROC-AUC.

\textbf{What breaks without it.} You would report one number per model and
invite exactly the comparison this domain cannot support --- ``model A is 0.47,
model B is 0.44'' --- with no statement of what either costs an analyst.

\aloud{Four numbers, and the third is the one people skip. This system doesn't
hand you a pile of work and wish you luck. You tell it what your team can get
through, and every number on the screen is measured at exactly that workload.}

\subsubsection*{The constellation}

\textbf{What it is.} Every alert as one dot. Size is group size, colour is risk
band, and any dot can be clicked straight into.

\textbf{What is real and what is not.} The dots, their sizes, their colours and
their ranks are real, taken live from the served queue. \textbf{The arrangement
is decorative.} Position carries no meaning, and saying so out loud is worth
more than the panel is: an evaluator who works that out unaided will start
wondering what else is decorative.

\textbf{Why it earns its space anyway.} It shows the \emph{shape} of the
problem in a way a table cannot --- a handful of hot cases and a long quiet
tail. That shape is the reason the review number matters.

\subsubsection*{The leaderboard}

Rank, risk score, member count, pattern name if there is one, and a jump arrow.
It is the shortest useful summary in the product, and the natural bridge into
the Alert Queue, which is the same information without the styling.

The ranked list an investigator actually works through.

\subsubsection*{The risk-band filter --- how to show a normal case}

Four buttons: \textbf{All}, \textbf{High risk}, \textbf{Medium},

\textbf{Why this matters in a presentation.} The first thing a sceptical

The ordinary groups were always there. On Elliptic, \textbf{148 of the 254

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Read the Mendeley row before a presentation --- somebody will ask.}
Every one of its 223 groups scores high, so the Low band is empty and the button
is greyed out with a zero on it.

That is not a bug, and it is not the model failing to recognise ordinary firms.
It follows directly from the dataset caveat in Section~\ref{sec:data}: Mendeley
is a \emph{study sample} in which about 42\% of rows are cartel cases by
construction. When that many entities in a pool are genuinely involved, almost
every group containing them looks suspicious --- correctly.

The honest answer if asked is: ``on that dataset there are no ordinary groups to
show, because the dataset was not built to contain many. For the contrast case,
look at the Bitcoin payments queue, where 148 of the 254 groups are ordinary.''
\end{warnbox}

The queue always fetches the whole list so these counts are true totals rather
than ``whatever fell inside the budget''. The \emph{budget} still governs what
the All view shows and what the precision readout reports --- that number has to
match the published protocol.

\subsubsection*{What each row shows --- the eight columns, in order}

These are the columns as they actually appear on screen, left to right. Hovering
any heading gives the same explanation inside the console.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\#} --- the rank. Position in the queue, worst first.
  \item \textbf{Risk} --- a genuine probability, because of the translation step,
        not an arbitrary number between 0 and 1.
  \item \textbf{Pattern} --- which of the nine known shapes this group matches,
        with its own symbol and colour. Reads \emph{``none named''} when a case
        file was written and no shape could be proved, and \emph{``not
        checked''} when no case file exists for that alert at all. See the
        warning below --- this column carried a real bug until 2026-07-27.
  \item \textbf{Flags} --- how many official indicators matched. Blank when none
        did.
  \item \textbf{Members} --- how many firms or accounts are in the group.
  \item \textbf{Activity} --- a tiny chart of activity over time. A flat line
        means everything happened in one window, which is common in the Bitcoin
        data. A varied line means genuine spread.
  \item \textbf{When} --- when it happened, and the format changes with the
        source. Contract data carries calendar years, so it reads \code{2015} or
        \code{2004 -- 2021}. \textbf{The Bitcoin data has no calendar dates at
        all} --- it is stamped with 49 numbered steps in order --- so it reads
        \code{step 35}. Until 2026-07-26 this column printed a bare
        \code{35 -- 35}, which a first-time viewer could not interpret and which
        looked like a fault rather than ``it all happened at once''.
  \item \textbf{Alert ID} --- the machine key. Hovering a row replaces it with
        three buttons: open the network, open the evidence sheet, ask the
        assistant.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{A real bug lived in this column, and an earlier draft of this report
defended it as correct behaviour. Both are worth knowing.}

\textbf{What was seen.} Every row of the Pattern column, on every dataset, was
empty.

\textbf{The wrong explanation (in this report until 2026-07-27).} That the
matcher had simply proved nothing --- it names a shape only when it can prove
one, so an empty column was honest. The measurement offered in support was
``0 of 254 Elliptic alerts carry a pattern''.

\textbf{What was actually true.} The queue is written by the ranking stage,
which runs \emph{before} the explanation stage. So \code{motif\_type} in
\code{alerts.parquet} is null on every row by construction --- including the
rows where the matcher later did prove a shape. Checking the bundles instead of
the queue: \textbf{12 of the first 40 Elliptic bundles carry \code{fan\_in} or
\code{fan\_out}}. The patterns existed all along; the value simply never reached
the alert row.

\textbf{The fix.} The serving layer now joins the two published artifacts at
read time, so a proven shape reaches the queue. On the live Elliptic queue that
turns 0 named patterns into \textbf{15 of the top 60}. Nothing is invented: the
name comes from the same bundle the explanations endpoint already serves. The
proper long-term fix is for the ranking stage to back-fill the column once
explanations exist, and that is recorded as an open item.

\textbf{The column now has three states, because two of them were being
conflated:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{named pattern} --- the matcher proved this shape;
  \item \textbf{``none named''} --- a case file was written and no shape could
        be proved. Honest, and common;
  \item \textbf{``not checked''} --- no case file exists for this alert at all,
        because full explanations are produced only for the head of the queue.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The lesson worth repeating.} A plausible story explained the symptom, it
was written into this report, and it was wrong. ``0 of 254'' should have been
read as suspicious rather than reassuring --- a rate of exactly zero across four
unrelated datasets is far more likely to be a broken join than a true finding.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Screen 3 --- Graph Explorer}

\textbf{What the screen is for.} It is the moment the argument becomes visible.
Every claim in this project rests on the idea that coordination has a geometry;
this screen is where somebody can look at that geometry rather than take our
word for it.

\subsubsection*{What you are looking at}

\textbf{Coral dots} are the flagged members of this group. Coral means
``flagged'' throughout the entire product and is used for nothing else, so it
never has to be explained twice. \textbf{Grey dots} are context: neighbours drawn
in so the group sits in a world rather than floating in space. \textbf{Lines}
are connections.

\textbf{Why context nodes are drawn at all.} Without them a flagged group looks
equally suspicious in every case, because you cannot see what it is
\emph{unusually} connected to. The contrast is the information.

\textbf{Widening the picture --- the \emph{Show} control.} Top right of the
screen are two buttons: \textbf{direct neighbours} draws the flagged group plus
everyone who deals with them directly, and \textbf{neighbours of neighbours}
also brings in who \emph{those} people deal with. On Elliptic alert 2 that is
the difference between 50 dots / 49 lines and 62 / 62.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Why not ``1 step out / 2 steps out''.} That was the first wording and it
was reported as confusing on sight --- reasonably, because \emph{step} already
means a unit of \emph{time} everywhere else in this product (``step 35'' in the
When column). Two meanings for one word in one screen is a defect, not a
quibble. The buttons now say what they draw.
\end{plain}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{A correction to an earlier draft of this report.} Until 2026-07-26 this
section claimed that \emph{``clicking a dot expands its own neighbours''}. It
never did. The service is alert-scoped rather than dot-scoped, so there is no
per-dot expansion to offer, and a reader who tried it found nothing happened.
The \emph{Context} buttons above are the real capability, and they were added
when the false claim was found. Claiming a feature the software does not have is
worse than having no feature.
\end{plain}

\textbf{Why the network is trimmed, and why we say so.} The server windows the
network to the alert's own members plus the chosen number of steps, inside the
alert's time range, before sending. A browser cannot draw a million connections,
and a silently cropped picture is worse than a small one because the viewer
assumes it is complete. When trimming happens, the screen says so.

\aloud{Coral is flagged. Grey is context, so the group sits in a world instead
of floating. And if we've had to trim the picture to keep it readable, it says
so on screen rather than quietly showing you a slice.}

\subsubsection*{The replay control, and the bug that produced it}

\textbf{What it does.} Plays the group's connections in order, so you watch the
case assemble itself.

\textbf{You will only ever see ONE mode at a time, and the data chooses it ---
not you.} There is no switch. The code looks at whether the connections in this
particular group carry different dates, and picks accordingly:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{replay flow} --- the dates differ, so the playhead follows real
        time. You get this on the contract datasets, where connections carry
        calendar years.
  \item \textbf{replay order} --- every connection shares one time step, so
        there is no time order to follow and the reveal follows the recorded
        sequence instead. You get this on most Bitcoin alerts.
\end{itemize}

The button label says which one is running, and \emph{beside it a sentence now
says why} --- for example ``in order --- every connection here shares one time
step, so there is no time order to show''. Before 2026-07-26 the button said only
``replay order'' with no explanation, so a first-time viewer had no idea a second
mode existed or that the choice was forced by the data.

\textbf{Why two modes exist.} Because of a real fault found on 2026-07-20.
Bitcoin connections inside one alert often all sit at a \emph{single} time step
--- we measured the top alert's 101 connections all at one moment. The original
replay animated strictly by date, so with every date identical the playhead
moved and nothing on screen changed. It looked broken, and the stakeholder
reported it as ``the replay does nothing''.

\textbf{Why we did not fix it by spreading the dates out.} That would have
invented data. The animation would have looked better and meant less. Instead
the code detects that timestamps do not vary, falls back to sequence order, and
renames the control so the viewer knows which thing they are watching.

\aloud{Watch the label on the button. Sometimes it replays real time passing.
Sometimes every connection in the group happened in the same window, so what
you're watching is sequence, not time --- and it says which.}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Why there are two replay modes} --- a real bug we found and fixed. Bitcoin
lines inside one alert often all share a \emph{single} time step. We measured it:
the top alert's 101 lines all sit at the same moment. The original replay
animated by date, so with every date identical nothing appeared to move and the
control looked broken. Rather than fake a spread of dates, we detect the
situation, switch to order, and \emph{say so on the screen}.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Screen 4 --- Case Detail}

\textbf{What the screen is for.} It is where a person decides. Everything before
it narrows the list; this screen has to carry enough for somebody to justify
opening an investigation --- or, just as often, to justify closing one.

\subsubsection*{The four panels, and what each is doing}

\textbf{Detected pattern.} A drawing of the shape that matched, if one did, plus
how many instances of it were found. \emph{Why a drawing:} the nine shapes are
geometric, and a picture of a rotation or a pass-through is understood
instantly where a definition is not.

\textbf{Red flags.} Cards quoting the official indicator text, each stamped with
its source and each showing why it matched \emph{here}. \emph{Why this panel is
the one to defend:} it is produced by hand-written rules, not by the learned
model, and the indicator wording is quoted from a fixed reference file. An
investigator who distrusts the neural network entirely can discard every other
panel and this one still stands.

\textbf{Evidence.} The facts themselves --- group size, connections between
members, time window, and amounts and fees where the dataset records them. Each
fact is labelled with where it came from. \emph{Why the source labels matter:}
without them a reader assumes one method produced everything, and the mixture
of learned scores, plain rules and statistical checks is precisely what makes
the case defensible. \emph{And if a fact is missing it is genuinely missing} ---
we never impute a value to fill a row, so on a dataset without amounts the
amounts row simply is not there.

\textbf{How solid is this?} Two plain questions about the evidence, answered Yes
or No, explained immediately below.

\textbf{Underneath all four:} a collapsible section holding every raw value in
the case file, an export button, and the fixed ethics wording. \emph{Why the raw
section exists:} the rule we set ourselves is that the presented version can
never contain less than the underlying file. Any field the dossier does not have
a designed panel for still appears there, by construction --- so a new field can
never be silently swallowed.

\aloud{Four panels. The shape, if we can prove one. The official warning signs,
which come from hand-written rules and not from the neural network. The facts,
each tagged with where it came from. And then the system marking its own
homework.}

\subsubsection*{Reading ``How solid is this?'' --- the two fidelity questions}

\textbf{First, what is being checked.} This panel is not about the whole group.
It is about a much smaller thing, and until you know what that thing is, the
rest of this subsection is unreadable.

When the system raises a case, it does two separate jobs. It gives the group a
risk score. And it picks out \textbf{a small handful of the connections inside
that group} --- often three or four out of hundreds --- and says: \emph{these
specific few are what my decision depended on.} Everything else in the group is
context.

That handful is what the dashboard calls \emph{``the few connections that
mattered most''}. Older drafts of this report called it ``the highlighted
links'' or ``the minimal subgraph'' without ever saying what it was, which made
the whole explanation collapse for anyone reading it cold.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{A worked example.} Case 16 on the Bitcoin data is a group of
\textbf{85 accounts} joined by \textbf{84 payments}. Out of all of that, the
system points at \textbf{3 accounts and the 4 payments between them}. So
``the handful'' here means those 4 payments, and nothing else.
\end{plain}

\textbf{Second, the two questions.} Once you know what the handful is, the two
obvious things to ask about it are the two things this panel asks. Both are
answered Yes or No.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Question 1 --- was that handful enough on its own?}

We run the case again, but this time we let the system see \emph{only} those
3 accounts and 4 payments. We hide the other 81 accounts completely.

If the same warning still comes out, the answer is \textbf{Yes}. That handful
really was enough by itself.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Question 2 --- was that handful actually necessary?}

We run the case a third time, with the full group present but those same
4 payments \emph{deleted}.

If the warning now disappears, the answer is \textbf{Yes}. Those payments
really were holding the case up.
\end{plain}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The everyday version.} Somebody says they failed an exam because of two
particular questions.

Test one: give them a paper containing \emph{only} those two questions. Do they
still fail? If yes, those two really were enough to sink them.

Test two: give them the full paper with those two questions \emph{removed}. Do
they now pass? If yes, those two really were the cause.

Answer Yes to both and the explanation is solid. Answer Yes then No --- which
is what most of our cases do --- and it means the handful is enough to
reproduce the decision, but the system could also have reached the same
conclusion by another route.
\end{keybox}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Why the panel does not just print the raw numbers} (a dashboard bug we
found and fixed on 2026-07-25). Underneath, these are two numbers called
\emph{fidelity$-$} and \emph{fidelity$+$}. For a single alert each is a hard 0 or
1, never a sliding scale --- and confusingly, \textbf{zero means opposite things
for the two}: fidelity$-$ of 0 is the \emph{best} possible result, while
fidelity$+$ of 0 is the \emph{weak} one.

The dashboard used to print ``fidelity plus 0.000 / fidelity minus 0.000'',
which told a viewer nothing and looked like a broken readout. It now asks the
question each number actually answers, colours the good answer, and keeps the
raw value in brackets for anyone who wants it.
\end{warnbox}

\subsubsection*{When an alert has no dossier at all}

Producing an explanation costs real computation, so bundles are assembled for
the \emph{head} of the queue only. Everything below that carries a score and a
network but no dossier. Measured across all four datasets: 980 alerts in total,
of which 93 have a bundle and 887 do not.

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{A fault we introduced and then fixed on 2026-07-25.} Adding the
``Low / normal'' filter made those lower-ranked groups reachable for the first
time --- and clicking one showed a red error box reading \emph{``No explanation
bundle for this alert''}. The system was behaving exactly as designed and
reporting it as a failure.

The screen now explains the situation instead. For a low-scoring group it says
plainly: \emph{``This group was scored as ordinary. The system did not consider
it worth a reviewer's time, so no detailed evidence was assembled. That is the
correct outcome --- it is the contrast case.''} Alongside it, everything we do
know is listed: rank, score, band, group size and time window, with a link into
the network view.
\end{warnbox}

\subsubsection*{When no pattern name appears}

Some alerts show ``no nameable motif matched''. That is honest, not a failure:
the matcher only names the nine shapes it can actually prove, and many real
communities do not contain a textbook shape. On the Mendeley queue the top
alerts are two-firm groups, which are too small to contain any shape at all.

In that case the dossier now describes the \emph{situation} in plain sentences,
built only from measured facts. How many are in the group. What arrangement
their connections are in. Whether it all happened in one stretch of time. And
which handful of them carries most of the case. Before 2026-07-25 that evidence
sat collapsed inside a technical appendix, which made those dossiers look empty
on exactly the alerts a reviewer most needs to justify.

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Three attempts at this wording, and what the last one changed.} The
first version read like a research paper and was rejected. The second passed a
14-term jargon test and was \emph{still} rejected --- a professional reviewer
said the language ``is not at all used in industry''.

The diagnosis on the third attempt was not vocabulary but \textbf{subject}.
Attempt two described \emph{the system}: ``we asked the computer which parts
actually made it suspicious''. Attempt three describes \emph{the situation}:
``the money went through all of them in one go, one after another''. Same
facts, same honesty rules, no mention of the machine at all.

The tests in \code{frontend/src/lib/plainReason.test.ts} now pin both bans ---
no machine-learning terms \emph{and} no words about the machine --- and cap
every sentence at 20 words.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Screen 5 --- Model Lab}

\textbf{What the screen is for.} It is where the claims live, and it is
deliberately ordered for presenting rather than for browsing: results first,
then the evidence that the results are trustworthy.

\textbf{One rule governs the whole screen: every number on it is copied from a
stored result file.} Nothing is computed in the browser. That is why a machine
without a particular artefact simply does not render that panel, instead of
rendering an estimate.

\subsubsection*{Top to bottom}

\textbf{1. The headline block, per run.} The overall score, the blind-guessing
level it must be read against, and the resulting ``$N\times$ better than
guessing'', with the size of the labelled test set underneath. \emph{Why the
guessing level is printed next to it and not in a footnote:} 0.39 on the
contracts data is a weaker result than 0.47 on the Bitcoin data, because their
guessing levels are 0.358 and 0.065. Quoting either score alone misleads, and
this is the single most common way results in this field are oversold.

\textbf{2. The workload table, per run.} One row per review size: precision,
recall and false-positive rate. \emph{Why it exists:} it is the table you take
to whoever signs off headcount, because it turns a research score into a
staffing decision. Only measured review sizes appear --- the table never
interpolates a row that was not evaluated.

\textbf{3. The charts.} Score by time period against the guessing line, with the
step-43 collapse visible; precision against workload with a live marker at
wherever the slider sits; and queue precision after near-duplicates are merged.
\emph{Why the collapse is not cropped out:} it is a real event in the data --- a
large market shut down and behaviour changed underneath the model --- and it is
one of our findings. It is also why the model is tested strictly forward in
time and why our validation scores are reported as an untrustworthy guide under
that kind of shift.

\textbf{4. The full tree,} collapsed: every raw number the run published, for
anyone who wants to check one.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Every chart carries its own instructions.} Underneath each one is a
collapsed line reading \emph{``How do I read this chart?''}. Opening it explains,
in plain words, what the horizontal axis is, what the vertical axis is, which
direction is good, and what the thing worth noticing is --- the period-43
collapse, the fact that precision is expected to fall as you review more, the
fact that the queue figure is a floor rather than a ceiling.

It is collapsed rather than printed because the marks should be read first, and
it sits below the chart rather than above it so it never competes with them. It
was added on 2026-07-26: a chart nobody can read is decoration, and the
difference between a figure and a finding is whether the reader knows which way
is up.
\end{plain}

\textbf{5. Rigour and uncertainty,} collapsed, at the bottom. Repeat runs with
their spread, the ``is the difference real or luck'' comparisons, the transfer
matrices, the label-noise curve and the protocol sweeps.

\begin{plain}
\textbf{Why rigour moved to the bottom.} It used to open the screen. A presenter
therefore hit seven repeat-run rows and a statistical readout before reaching a
single headline number, which is the wrong order for a talk: ``can I trust
this?'' is a follow-up question, not an opening one. It is collapsed rather than
removed, and it is one click away, because when somebody does ask it the answer
has to be immediate.
\end{plain}

\aloud{Every number on this screen comes out of a stored file --- nothing here is
worked out in the browser. And no score ever appears on its own. It always sits
next to what blind guessing would have got you on the same data.}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{A clutter bug fixed on 2026-07-25.} This screen used to dump
\emph{every} numeric field a run published as its own small tile, in whatever
order they happened to sit in the file --- twelve unlabelled tiles per run, two
runs per dataset. It was impossible to present from.

It also formatted every one of them to four decimal places, so the count of
test items rendered as ``16670.0000''.

Now: one headline number that carries its own meaning, and one ordered table.
The underlying numbers are identical --- only the arrangement changed.
\end{warnbox}

Everything exports as an image for papers and slides.

\textbf{What the screen is for.} It is the argument in one page, and it is the

\textbf{What is on it.} The ``two ledgers, one shape'' thesis. Then the nine

\subsection{The assistant, and the knowledge base behind it}

Ask questions in ordinary English, such as ``why was alert 3 flagged?''.

\subsubsection*{Where its answers come from}

The assistant is not allowed to answer from memory. Before it writes anything it
must go and look something up. It has exactly three places it is allowed to
look, and nothing else:

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{4.3cm}p{11.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Source} & \textbf{What it holds} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{The knowledge base} & Two parts. \emph{(a) The warning-sign library:} the
FATF (money laundering) and OECD (bid rigging) indicator tables, plus the notes
saying which shape triggers which indicator --- each with a citable id such as
\code{FATF-STRUCT-01}. \emph{(b) The project glossary:} our own vocabulary, so
the assistant can also explain \emph{our} words --- risk score, review budget,

\textbf{The results database} & The stored alerts, scores and metrics ---

\subsubsection*{How the knowledge base works}

\begin{plain}
\pw{Think of a small, carefully written reference book about fraud warning
signs. When you ask a question, the assistant looks up the handful of pages most
relevant to your words, reads only those, and must quote a page number for
anything it claims.}

Technically this is called \textbf{retrieval}: the question is matched against
the library using a classic word-overlap ranking method (\textbf{BM25}), which
scores a passage by how many of your rare words it contains. The matched entries
come back with their ids, and the grounding check then verifies that every claim
in the answer traces to one of them.
\end{plain}

\textbf{Why a small curated library rather than a giant one?} Three reasons, all
practical. It is \emph{license-safe} --- the indicator texts are our own
paraphrased condensations, not copied source material. It is \emph{citable} ---
every entry has a stable id, which is exactly what the grounding check needs. And
it is \emph{small}, so a simple word-overlap search is genuinely enough; we did
not need a heavyweight vector database, and adding one would have been
complexity for its own sake.

\subsubsection*{The glossary layer --- why we added it}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{A gap the query battery exposed.} Asked \emph{``What does the risk score
mean?''} the assistant replied with polite filler --- ``I'm ready to help with any
questions you have'' --- and nothing else.

The cause was structural, not a bug in the model. The library defined
\textbf{crime patterns} (what a cover bid is, what structuring looks like) and
nothing at all defined \textbf{our own words}. So when someone asked about the
risk score there was nothing to retrieve, nothing to cite, and the grounding
rule correctly refused to let it invent an answer. It failed safe --- but it
failed.

\textbf{Fix:} we wrote a glossary of our own terms and added it to the library.
It covers the words a reviewer actually sees on screen: risk score, risk band,
review budget, precision, AUC-PR, prevalence, alert, motif, fidelity,
explanation bundle, community, anti-money laundering, bid rigging, the four
outcomes (TP/TN/FP/FN), and the screening-not-guilt rule.

Each one can be looked up and quoted by name, exactly like an official
indicator.

Asked the same question afterwards, it now answers properly: a calibrated
probability between 0 and 1, a screening signal and not a determination of
guilt, with the alert record and the caveat quoted as evidence.
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{One rule these entries must follow:} a definition may never state a
\emph{measured number}. Numbers come from the tools; meanings come from the
glossary. If a definition hard-coded a score, the assistant could quote it and
the numeric check would flag it as unsupported --- and worse, the number would
silently go stale. A test enforces this: it rejects any glossary entry
containing a measured-looking value. It caught one on the first run, in an
example sentence we had written ourselves.

\subsubsection*{What makes it safe}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It can only read.} It cannot change anything.
  \item \textbf{Grounding.} Every number it states must appear in the data it just
        looked up. Unsupported claims are blocked, not printed.
  \item \textbf{Accusation guard.} Accusing phrasing is rewritten or blocked before
        release. Measured: \textbf{zero violations got through} across the full
        test set of questions.
  \item \textbf{24 standard questions} are run as a gate. All 24 pass.
  \item \textbf{Every answer is labelled as AI-written} and carries the caveat.
\end{itemize}

\emph{likely} to ask --- the assistant produced \textbf{168 seconds of repeated
gibberish} (\code{<unk><unk><unk>\ldots}) and displayed it.

released the result without checking it.

running a grounding test over gibberish is meaningless. It catches repeated
tokens, any short fragment repeated sixteen times or more, near-zero variety,
and empty replies. When it fires, the assistant says it could not produce a
reliable answer and withholds the text, at zero confidence. Two tests pin the
behaviour, including protection against false alarms on legitimate repetitive
formatting such as tables and bullet lists.

arithmetic worked out \emph{from} verified numbers is sometimes wrongly flagged
as unsupported (which lowers the confidence badge for no good reason); and very
open-ended list questions exhaust the tool budget.

\section{Who Would Actually Use This}

\subsection{A realistic day}

\begin{plain}
An analyst at a national audit office can review about 20 cases a week. Last
quarter there were 40{,}000 contracts.

\textbf{Without this system:} she filters by simple rules --- single-bid contracts,
unusual values. The list is thousands long and mostly boring. Our own measurement
showed rule-based filters ranking \emph{worse than random} on the money data.

\textbf{With this system:} she sets the slider to 20. She gets 20 ranked
\emph{groups}, each with a named pattern, an official warning-sign quote, and the
specific evidence. She opens the first: five firms, a rotation pattern, they have
never bid against each other, and two share a registered address. She reads the
evidence, asks the assistant to summarise the timeline, exports the file, and
escalates it to an investigator.

\textbf{She still makes every decision.} The system changed \emph{what she looked
at first}. It did not decide anything.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Who should not use it, and for what}

\begin{warnbox}
\textbf{Not for:} deciding guilt; automatic punishment of any kind such as
blacklisting or freezing accounts; evidence in court; producing any output about
a named private individual; blocking transactions in real time.

This is a \textbf{prioritisation aid} and a research prototype. It has no login
system, no protection for multiple organisations sharing it, no case-management
workflow, and no real-time operation.

Fraud screening is treated as high-risk under European AI regulation, with
obligations that become enforceable in August 2026. Our design --- a human always
decides, every alert is explained, every output carries its caveat --- is our
documented position on that. It is not a certificate of compliance.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Limitations --- Stated Plainly}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{XGBoost beats our graph model at sorting individual Bitcoin
        transactions} (0.8104 against 0.4729). Section~\ref{sec:isitdl} explains
        why and puts it in proportion, but the fact stands.
  \item \textbf{Our contract-side graph model scores below the guessing level}
        (0.2808 against 0.358), consistently. A genuine weakness.
  \item \textbf{Validation cannot be trusted here.} It says 0.93--0.95 while the
        real score is 0.42--0.55, and corrupting the training answers
        \emph{improves} the test score. We currently have no reliable way to pick
        the best model under changing conditions.
  \item \textbf{Attempt-to-attempt variation is large} ($\pm$0.05), bigger than
        several effects we would like to claim.
  \item \textbf{Mendeley percentages are study artifacts}, so only
        ``times better than guessing'' comparisons are meaningful there.
  \item \textbf{Bitcoin features are anonymised}, so money-side explanations lean
        on structure and timing rather than meaning.
  \item \textbf{No learned explanation for the contract-side model.} The explainer
        technique only works on certain model types.
  \item \textbf{No human has judged the explanations yet.} The study is built and
        tested but has not been run, so ``are these explanations useful to real
        investigators?'' is not yet answered with evidence.
  \item \textbf{Shape-based screening is blind to rotation, market-splitting and
        cover bidding} --- measured, and consistent across all attempts.
  \item \textbf{AMLworld needs an edge-level model, which we have not built.}
        The dataset runs here fine (52 seconds), but because its answer key
        marks payments rather than accounts, our account-level models score
        \emph{below} blind guessing on it (0.60--0.62$\times$). Fixing this
        means scoring payments, not accounts.
  \item \textbf{Not a product:} no login, no multi-organisation support, no
        real-time path.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\subsection{Fixing the limitations above}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Find a validation method that survives changing conditions.} This
        is the single most important open problem --- limitation 3 above.
  \item \textbf{Close the gap with XGBoost honestly.} Either find a design that
        genuinely beats it on Elliptic, or map out precisely when graph learning
        pays. Our own evidence already suggests the answer: it pays when the
        neighbour information is \emph{not} already copied into the spreadsheet.
  \item \textbf{Explanations for the contract-side model}, closing that gap.
  \item \textbf{Run the human study} with at least five reviewers and report how
        much they agree with each other.
  \item \textbf{Add price and timing detectors} to catch the cartels that shape
        alone provably misses. This is the clearest scientific gap we found.
  \item \textbf{Score payments rather than accounts on AMLworld}, using models
        that carry information along the payment itself (PNA, GIN with edge
        updates). This is now a concrete, measured task rather than a vague
        ``needs hardware'' item --- the data runs here in under a minute; only
        the model is missing.
  \item \textbf{Try continuous-time models} (TGN, TGAT) to see whether they beat
        our snapshot approach.
  \item \textbf{Build firm-to-firm co-bidding links before grouping} in the García
        data, which should give a richer contract queue than the current honest
        but thin 3 alerts.
  \item \textbf{Production hardening:} logins, multi-organisation support,
        incremental scoring, and integration with case-management systems.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Should we add blockchain or Web3? An honest assessment}
\label{sec:web3}

This deserves a real answer rather than an enthusiastic one. We judged every idea
by one test: \emph{does it solve a problem we actually have?}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{First, something easy to miss:} this project is \textbf{already} doing
blockchain analysis. The Elliptic dataset \emph{is} Bitcoin. We are already
running graph forensics on a blockchain ledger.

So the real question is not ``should we touch Web3''. It is ``which
\emph{additional} Web3 capabilities earn their place''.
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection*{Where it genuinely adds value}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Reading the chain live (the strongest case).} Today the money side
        looks backwards at a frozen snapshot from years ago. A live feed would
        make it work \emph{as things happen}. This is a real gain, and our design
        already supports it --- it is a new data reader feeding the same shared
        format. The honest catch is that our pipeline currently works in nightly
        batches, so this also needs scoring that updates continuously.
  \item \textbf{Following money across different blockchains.} Moving assets
        between chains through ``bridges'' is now a major laundering route --- and
        it is exactly a network problem, because a bridge is simply a line joining
        two networks. Our shared format already supports different kinds of line,
        and R-GCN already handles them, so this is a natural extension rather than
        a bolt-on.
  \item \textbf{New shapes from decentralised finance.} Mixing services, fake
        trading in digital collectibles, and circular instant-loan structures are
        new \emph{shapes} --- and shapes are precisely what this system detects.
        They would slot into the existing shape table and matcher with no
        architectural change. This is the most natural fit of all.
  \item \textbf{Networks of smart contracts calling each other.} A rich,
        under-explored network, and a genuine third domain to test the ``one
        system, many crimes'' claim.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Where it would be decoration --- and one place it would do harm}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Publishing alerts onto a blockchain: no, and we should say so
        firmly.} This clashes directly with the project's central ethical rule.
        Our alerts are a signal to look closer, not a finding of guilt, and they
        can be wrong --- our own measurements say so. A blockchain is permanent and
        public by design. Publishing ``firm X is flagged'' to a permanent public
        record would create an unerasable accusation against someone convicted of
        nothing. That is the exact opposite of what our ethics statement requires,
        and no efficiency argument outweighs it.
  \item \textbf{Paying people in tokens for reporting suspicion: no.} Reward people
        per flag and you manufacture false alarms. Our entire design fights false
        alarms under a fixed budget. A token reward would fight the design.
  \item \textbf{Training the model across organisations via a blockchain: not
        justified here.} That technique solves ``the data cannot leave the
        building''. Every dataset we use is already public. We would pay a large
        complexity cost to solve a problem we do not have. (It \emph{would} matter
        if real banks ever pooled private data --- but the technology needed then
        is federated learning, which does not require a blockchain.)
  \item \textbf{Digital collectibles, token voting, or a project coin: no.} Nothing
        here needs transferable ownership or on-chain voting.
  \item \textbf{A blockchain as a tamper-proof audit trail: valid, but the wrong
        tool.} Being able to prove which model version produced an alert is a real
        requirement. We already meet it more simply: versioned result folders,
        locked configurations, fingerprinted inputs, and reproducibility verified
        across three computers. If we ever needed stronger guarantees, a signed
        append-only log gives the same tamper-evidence without a blockchain's
        cost, delay and governance overhead.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Recommendation.} Pursue Web3 \emph{as a source of data}, not as
infrastructure. Ideas 1--4 are worth real effort because they feed the network
engine we already have. Everything in the second list should be declined openly
--- and publishing alerts on-chain should be declined on \emph{ethical} grounds,
which is a stronger and more defensible position than a technical objection.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{What the system writes down}

Every run writes a machine-readable record. These are the logs.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{run.json} --- the full settings, the random seed, how many rounds it
        trained, the best round, the best validation score, how long it took, and
        every resulting number.
  \item \code{metrics.json} --- the evaluation results, broken down by time step.
  \item \code{scores\_test.parquet} --- the raw score for every dot.
  \item \code{model.pt} and \code{feature\_stats.json} --- the trained model and the
        locked rescaling amounts.
  \item \code{multiseed.json} --- all five attempts plus their average and spread.
  \item \code{alerts.parquet} and the explanation files --- the queue and the
        evidence.
  \item \code{BUILD\_REPORT.json} --- which report tables were built, and for any
        that were skipped, exactly which file was missing.
\end{itemize}

\begin{plain}
\textbf{A real log record} (Elliptic, GATv2 with focal loss, attempt 0):
model GATv2, raw features, focal loss, 64 hidden units, 2 layers, 4 attention
heads, 30\% dropout. \textbf{It trained for 142 rounds, and the best was round
121.} Best validation score 0.9497. Training took 2194.6 seconds. Final test score
0.5492, against a guessing level of 0.0650.

\textbf{Look at the gap} between validation (0.9497) and test (0.5492). That one
log line \emph{is} limitation 3 --- visible in the raw record.
\end{plain}

\subsection{Reproducibility}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{docs/reproducibility.md} maps \textbf{all 40 experiment
        configurations} to the exact numbers they produce. An automatic test
        enforces this both ways: a configuration missing from the document, or a
        document entry with no configuration, fails the build.
  \item The software versions are locked.
  \item \code{uv run poe paper-tables} rebuilds every table from stored results. A
        table whose source file is missing is \textbf{skipped, with the missing
        path recorded} --- never drawn from partial data. \textbf{Currently all 10
        tables build with nothing skipped.}
  \item \textbf{Checked across computers:} the tree results, the country-transfer
        result (0.8025340470101002, to the last digit), the market matrix, and the
        Georgian data processing all reproduce \emph{identically} on three
        different machines. Deep-learning results reproduce exactly on the same
        machine and vary by at most $\pm$0.02 across different machines --- smaller
        than the $\pm$0.05 variation between attempts, which is why we only ever
        publish the average of five.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{We attacked our own work on purpose}

We ran a formal internal attempt to break our own results, checking for leaks,
misleading reporting, unfair comparisons, inconsistent numbers, invalid
explanations, and ethics gaps.

\textbf{Result: nothing found invalidated any published number.} Two presentation
faults were found and fixed. The main one: single-attempt comparison figures were
printed next to five-attempt averages, so a reader recomputing the difference
would get a number outside the stated range. Both are now labelled explicitly,
and our table-building code now keeps the two kinds apart automatically.

\section{Questions an Examiner Will Ask}

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{5.0cm}p{10.5cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Answer} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\emph{XGBoost beat your deep model. Is this still a deep learning project?} &
Yes. XGBoost wins one of seven jobs, and only because that dataset already has
the network information copied into its spreadsheet --- proven by the fact that
adding more network information changes the score by just +0.0028. It cannot do
the other six jobs at all, and it is not in the shipped product. Full audit in
Section~\ref{sec:isitdl}. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{How do I know nothing leaked?} & Four independent safeguards: we hide
test-period \emph{lines}, not just answers (checked: 77{,}512 withheld); features
use only what was known at the time (checked: exactly equal to the properly cut
network); 25 automatic tests run on every change; and our own review \emph{found}
a leak and cut our headline by 23\% to fix it. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Isn't your ``correct alert'' rule too generous?} & On its own, yes. That is
why it comes with a 100-member cap, duplicate removal, and a re-run under much
stricter rules requiring 10\% and 25\% of members confirmed. Results held. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Why AUC-PR and not ROC-AUC?} & With 2 criminals in 100, ROC-AUC mostly
rewards sorting the 98 innocent cases, which is easy. Worked example in
Section~\ref{sec:metrics}. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Did the baselines get the same tuning effort?} & Yes --- precisely, because
neither side got any tuning search at all. That is conservative against us, since
the trees win every comparison they enter. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Are the differences real or luck?} & Tested with a paired bootstrap over
2{,}000 re-samples of the same cases, reported with ranges, and labelled as
single-attempt wherever they sit beside five-attempt averages. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Only one run?} & Never for a headline. Always five. The 0.5492 we once
quoted was the luckiest of five; the average is 0.4729. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Are the explanations real or decoration?} & Measured, which is why we
\emph{replaced} the first explainer: it failed sanity checks on 41 of 50 alerts.
The current one fails 1 of 50. Human judging is still pending and we say so. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Does it transfer to new places?} & Partly, and we publish the failures: one
dataset works on every market (1.57 times better than guessing); the other depends
on the country, and its largest country \emph{fails} at 0.90. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{What can it not catch?} & Rotation, market-splitting and cover bidding ---
measured on 100 planted cartels in a 163{,}000-dot network, consistent across all
attempts, because shape alone cannot see them. \\
\addlinespace
\emph{Could this harm someone?} & The rule is enforced in code, not merely stated:
a caveat field the software refuses to let anyone alter, the caveat on every
response and screen, and an assistant that rewrites accusing language (zero got
through). No output about any named individual exists anywhere in the system. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\section{Summary}

\begin{keybox}
CollusionGraph turns two different crimes into the same question --- \emph{which
connected group is behaving like a team?} --- and answers it with a short, ranked,
explained list that a human reviews.

\textbf{What works}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Groups that bid together are found reliably at large scale with no answer
        key at all. Score 0.9225, reviewing just 1.2\% of the network. The
        non-learning method gets 0.1237.
  \item Translating scores before combining them is a measured requirement, not a
        preference.
  \item Moving to an unseen market works on one dataset family. On the other it
        depends on the country, and we publish which ones fail.
  \item Explanations are tested, not assumed.
  \item The whole thing reproduces exactly across three computers.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What does not}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A tree method sorts individual transactions better than our graph model
        --- on the one dataset that hands it the answer for free.
  \item Our contract-side graph model scores below guessing.
  \item Validation cannot be trusted when conditions change.
  \item Shape alone is blind to take-turns cartels.
\end{itemize}

Publishing both lists is the point. A screening tool whose limits are unknown
cannot be used by anyone who has to answer for the decision.
\end{keybox}
